\begin{abstract}
We use the representation of data types as container functors to obtain a decision
procedure for equality of a large class of polymorphic functions on lists.  A container
describes a data type by the \emph{shapes} its values can take and, for each shape, the
\emph{positions} at which data are stored; a representation theorem guarantees that every
polymorphic function between container types is given by a map on shapes and a family of
maps on positions.  For lists we show that the dependently typed position maps can be
replaced by functions on the natural numbers, and that when shape and position maps are definable in Presburger
arithmetic, equality of polymorphic functions reduces to the validity of universal
Presburger formulae.  The procedure is implemented as a Coq tactic that proves the
standard identities between list functions without induction.
We then give the same mathematics a second reading.  A container is also the type of an
\emph{interface}: the moves an agent can emit and, for each move, the responses it
accepts; container morphisms are \emph{adapters} between interfaces (dependent lenses).
Interfaces whose moves are lists --- batches of tool calls, context windows, ranked
candidates --- are list-shaped, and the adapters that truncate, reorder, split and merge
them are list morphisms of the kind our procedure decides.  We prove that for such
adapters arithmetic equality coincides with equality of container morphisms and with
observational equivalence: two adapters are equal exactly when every agent wrapped by
them produces the same runs against every environment.  Losslessness of an adapter is
decidable too, and both properties lift to lists of typed items and transfer to
tree-shaped plans.  All results are mechanised in an axiom-free Coq development.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

Techniques for reasoning about polymorphic functions have applications that range from
specification and verification, through model checking and testing, to the certification
of mobile code.  This paper presents a way of reasoning about polymorphic functions that
is based on the representation of data types as \emph{containers}
\cite{Abbott03,AbbottAltenkirchGhani03,AbbottAltenkirchGhani05}.  A container describes a
concrete data type by two pieces of information: the \emph{shapes} that values of the
type can take, and, for each shape, the set of \emph{positions} at which data are stored.
A list, for instance, has as shape its length $n$ and as positions the $n$ slots
$0,\dots,n-1$; a binary tree with data at its leaves has as shape the tree with the data
erased, and as positions the leaves of that tree.  A value of the type is then a shape
together with an assignment of a datum to each position.

Earlier presentations of container theory used the language of category theory
\cite{AbbottAltenkirchGhani03,AbbottAltenkirchGhani05} and investigated its applications
to generic programming \cite{AltenkirchMcBrideMorris07,Morris07}.  Our interest comes from
automated reasoning, and is motivated by the ability of containers to simplify reasoning
tasks \cite{AbbottAltenkirchGhaniMcBride04,PrinceGhaniMcBride08}.  The basis of everything
that follows is the \emph{representation theorem} for containers: every polymorphic
function (natural transformation) between container types is given by a map on shapes
and, contravariantly, a family of maps on positions.  For instance, a polymorphic function
$F : \List\,X \to \List\,X$ is represented by a pair
\[
  \big(\;u : \N \to \N,\quad f : \textstyle\prod_{n:\N}\, \Fin\,(u\,n) \to \Fin\,n\;\big),
\]
where $u$ computes the length of the output from the length of the input, and, for each
input length $n$, the \emph{reindexing map} $f_n$ says from which position of the input
each position of the output is fetched.  Here $\Fin\,n$ is the type with exactly $n$
elements, which we think of as $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$.  Reversal, for example, keeps the length
($u\,n = n$) and fetches output position $i$ from input position $n - i - 1$.

Many functions on lists are, in this representation, nothing but arithmetic on lengths and
indices.  This was the insight behind Bundy and Richardson's \emph{ellipsis}
technique \cite{BundyRichardson99}, in which lists are written as
$x_1,\dots,x_n$ and functions are specified by what they do to indices, and it was
developed further, with containers as the formal underpinning, in
\cite{PrinceGhaniMcBride08}.  Those developments were carried out in a dependently typed
setting, where indices live in the finite types $\Fin\,n$.  Dependent types are what make
the representation \emph{correct by construction} --- an index into a list of length $n$
cannot exceed $n-1$ --- but they are also what prevents the direct use of decision
procedures, which operate on natural numbers or integers, not on families of finite types.

\subsection{Contribution: arithmetic verification}

The first contribution of this paper is to show that, for lists, nothing is lost by
abandoning dependent types at the point where decision procedures are needed.  The
behaviour of a reindexing map $f : \prod_n \Fin(u\,n) \to \Fin\,n$ is captured by a
function $g : \N \times \N \to \N$ on natural numbers together with a side condition
(the index is in range), and equality of two polymorphic functions becomes a statement of
the form
\[
  \forall n.\; u\,n = v\,n \;\wedge\; \forall n\,i.\; i < u\,n \to g_1\,n\,i = g_2\,n\,i .
\]
When $u$, $v$, $g_1$ and $g_2$ are definable in Presburger arithmetic --- the first-order
theory of the natural numbers with addition, which is decidable \cite{Presburger29} --- so
is this statement, and because it has a universal prefix and a quantifier-free matrix it
falls into the fragment handled efficiently by the Omega test \cite{Pugh91}, by Coq's
\coq{lia} tactic and by SMT solvers.  We identify a class of
\emph{Presburger-definable morphisms}, closed under composition, that covers the
familiar functions on lists (\S\ref{sec:arith}), and we implement a Coq tactic that proves
the identities of Figure~\ref{fig:properties} automatically.

\subsection{Contribution: typed agent interfaces}

The second contribution was prompted by an observation of Ghani \cite{GhaniPodcast26}.
An \emph{AI agent} responds to prompts: there is a type of prompts and, for every prompt,
a type of possible responses --- and that is a container.  The whole theory of containers
--- and its continuation as the theory of polynomial functors by Spivak and
Niu \cite{Spivak20,NiuSpivak25} --- therefore applies to the \emph{interfaces} of agents.
The same container can be read from either side of an interface.  The party that answers
sees a prompt arrive and must produce a response of the type that the prompt dictates; the
party that asks emits a \emph{move} and then accepts a response whose type depends on the
move.  We take the second reading as primary, because it is the one in which a stateful
agent is a coalgebra (\S\ref{sec:agents}), and we call the answering party the
\emph{environment}; nothing depends on the choice, because the notion of morphism is the
same.  A container morphism $\cont{S}{P} \lens \cont{S'}{P'}$ is a protocol
\emph{adapter}: it translates the moves of one interface into those of another
(covariantly) and routes responses back (contravariantly).  In the terminology of
\cite{NiuSpivak25} it is a \emph{dependent lens}.  Composition of adapters is composition
of container morphisms, and wrapping an agent by an adapter is the extension of the
container morphism applied to the agent's one-step behaviour.

Most of today's agentic systems are untyped: a language model has, as Ghani puts it, the
type $\fn{String} \to \fn{String}$, and orchestration frameworks pass around lists of
messages and JSON objects whose shapes are checked, if at all, at run time.  A
\emph{typed} agentic system, of the kind being built at Kodamai
\cite{KodamaiEEJ26,GhaniPodcast26}, assigns container types to interfaces and composes
agents along typed adapters.  Once that is done, a familiar dividend of types becomes
available: to check that two ways of wiring a system agree, or that an adapter preserves
information, one checks the types and the adapters, not the agents.

Here is where the first contribution meets the second.  A great many agent interfaces are
\emph{list-shaped}: an orchestrator that fans out $n$ tool calls and collects their
results; a conversation whose context is a window over a history of length $n$; a
retriever that returns $k$ ranked documents.  Adapters between list-shaped interfaces
are built from list morphisms --- truncate, drop the oldest, reverse the ranking, split
and merge a batch --- and these are Presburger-definable morphisms in the sense of
\S\ref{sec:arith}.  Consequently, (i) equality of two adapter pipelines is decidable;
(ii) losslessness of an adapter (no response dropped or duplicated) is decidable; and
(iii) equality of adapters is \emph{observational equivalence}: two adapters are equal if
and only if every agent wrapped by them produces the same runs against every
environment, so that the procedure decides how adapters behave and not merely how they
are written.  We prove these facts in Coq (\S\ref{sec:agents-verif}) and verify, in about
a second or less each, invariants of sliding context windows --- with and without a
pinned system prompt --- and of fan-out/fan-in routing; just as usefully, several
plausible-looking adapter laws are \emph{refuted}, by a verified search that computes the
counterexamples.

\subsection{Relation to earlier work}

The arithmetic core (\S\ref{sec:containers}--\S\ref{sec:arith}) develops ideas first set
out in the author's thesis \cite{Prince10} and, in dependently typed form, in
\cite{PrinceGhaniMcBride08}.  Relative to those, the decidability theorem is given a proof
(Theorem~\ref{thm:decidable}), correctness of composition is stated and proved
(Theorem~\ref{thm:comp}), the arithmetic representation is connected to container
morphisms proper and shown to be exact for them (Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge}), and the Coq
development has been written afresh: it is self-contained, axiom-free, uses \coq{lia} in place of the retired
\coq{omega}, proves each identity of Figure~\ref{fig:properties} in a fraction of a
second, and connects the container-style definitions with ordinary recursive definitions
through \emph{transfer lemmas}, from which standard theorems about lists follow without
induction (\S\ref{sec:coq}).\footnote{The Coq sources that accompanied \cite{Prince10} are
kept in the \texttt{legacy} folder of the repository given in \S\ref{sec:notation}.}  The
interface reading (\S\ref{sec:agents} and \S\ref{sec:agents-verif}) is new.

\subsection{Notation and conventions}\label{sec:notation}

We use two notations side by side, and we say which is which.  Mathematical statements and
definitions are written in a type-theoretic notation in the style of Agda and
Epigram \cite{McBrideMcKinna04}: $\sum_{s:S} P\,s$ (or $\sum s : S.\, P\,s$) is the type of
dependent pairs $(s, p)$ with $s : S$ and $p : P\,s$; $\prod_{s:S} P\,s$ is the type of
dependent functions $f$ with $f\,s : P\,s$ for every $s$; and a definition by pattern
matching lists one clause per constructor, with the \emph{absurd clause} ``$!$''
marking a case that cannot arise (written \verb|()| in Agda).  Code from the accompanying
development is shown in Coq syntax in typewriter font.  In Coq, $\sum$ is \coq{sigT} or
\coq{\{x : A \& B x\}} and $\prod$ is \coq{forall}.  We write $S\,n$ for the successor of
$n$ in pattern-matching clauses and $n + 1$ in arithmetic; the two are identical.
Cut-off subtraction on $\N$, written $n \monus m$ and equal to $0$ when $m \ge n$, is
the subtraction of Coq's \coq{nat}.  Lists are indexed from $0$ throughout.

\paragraph{Accompanying development.}  All definitions and results marked
\textsf{[Coq]} are formalised in the development (directory \texttt{theories/}) at
\url{https://github.com/tucananalytics/AAPP}, of which \S\ref{sec:coq} gives an overview.
Results not so marked are stated and proved in the text only.

\section{Containers}\label{sec:containers}
% ===========================================================================

We begin with an informal motivation, then give the definitions we need.  Readers familiar
with containers may skip to \S\ref{sec:rep-eq}; readers wishing to see the full theory
should consult \cite{AbbottAltenkirchGhani05}.

\subsection{Motivation: unique representations of lists}\label{sec:motivation}

Bundy and Richardson \cite{BundyRichardson99} represent a list of elements of type
$\tau$ by a length and a function that produces its elements:
\begin{align*}
  \Box &: \N \to (\N \to \tau) \to \List\,\tau \\
  \Box\,(0, f) &= [\,] \\
  \Box\,(S\,n, f) &= f\,1 :: \Box\,(n, f \circ S).
\end{align*}
Thus $\Box(n, f)$ is the list $[f\,1, f\,2, \dots, f\,n]$, and functions on lists can
often be written as arithmetic on $n$ and on the arguments of $f$.  The difficulty with
this representation is that $\Box$ is not injective: the lists $\Box(2, f)$ and
$\Box(2, f')$ are equal as soon as $f$ and $f'$ agree on $1$ and $2$, whatever they do
elsewhere, so a list has many representations and $f$ carries information that is never
observed.  The cure is to restrict the domain of $f$ to the positions that exist, that is,
to make its domain depend on $n$.  Using the finite types
\begin{lstlisting}
Inductive Fin : nat -> Set :=
  | fz : forall n, Fin (S n)
  | fs : forall n, Fin n -> Fin (S n).
\end{lstlisting}
(so $\Fin\,0$ is empty, and $\Fin(S\,n)$ has the elements $\fn{fz}$ and $\fn{fs}\,i$ for
$i : \Fin\,n$ --- we write $\fn{fs}$ in italics to avoid reading it as an application
$f\,s$), the type
\[
  \Phi\,X \;=\; \textstyle\sum_{n:\N}\, (\Fin\,n \to X)
\]
represents lists uniquely: a pair $(n, f)$ with $f : \Fin\,n \to X$ determines the list
$[f\,0, \dots, f\,(n-1)]$ and is determined by it.  $\Phi$ is a functor, with
$\Phi\,g\,(n, f) = (n, g \circ f)$.  It turns out that $\Phi$ is precisely the
representation of lists that container theory produces.

\subsection{Containers and their extensions}

\begin{definition}[Container]\label{def:container}
  A \emph{container} $\cont{S}{P}$ consists of a type $S$ of \emph{shapes} and, for each
  shape $s : S$, a type $P\,s$ of \emph{positions}.
\end{definition}

We sometimes write a container in pointwise form, $(s : S \triangleleft P\,s)$, to make
the shape variable explicit.  The \emph{list container} is
$(n : \N \triangleleft \Fin\,n)$: a shape is a length and the positions of a list of
length $n$ are its $n$ slots.  The container of binary trees with data at the leaves has
as shapes the trees with the data erased and as positions, for each such tree, its leaves
\cite{AbbottAltenkirchGhaniMcBride04}; we return to it in \S\ref{sec:trees}.

\begin{definition}[Extension]\label{def:extension}
  The \emph{extension} of a container $\cont{S}{P}$ is the functor
  $\ext{\cont{S}{P}} : \Set \to \Set$ defined by
  \[
    \ext{\cont{S}{P}}\,X \;=\; \textstyle\sum_{s:S}\, (P\,s \to X),
  \]
  with action on functions $\ext{\cont{S}{P}}\,g\,(s,h) = (s, g \circ h)$.
\end{definition}

A value of $\ext{\cont{S}{P}}\,X$ is a shape together with an assignment of an element of
$X$ to every position of that shape.  For the list container,
$\ext{(\N \triangleleft \Fin)}\,X = \sum_n (\Fin\,n \to X) = \Phi\,X \cong \List\,X$.

\begin{remark}[Calculating shapes]\label{rem:shapes}
  The shapes of an algebraic data type can be read off by setting the payload type to the
  unit type: the shape type of $\List\,X$ is $\List\,\mathbf{1} \cong \N$ and that of
  $\fn{Btree}\,X$ is $\fn{Btree}\,\mathbf{1}$.  Containers are closed under the
  constructions of strictly positive types, given by the grammar
  \[
    \tau \;::=\; X \;\mid\; \mathbf{0} \;\mid\; \mathbf{1} \;\mid\; \tau + \tau \;\mid\;
    \tau \times \tau \;\mid\; K \to \tau \;\mid\; \mu X.\,\tau ,
  \]
  in which $X$ ranges over type variables, $\mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{1}$ are the empty and
  unit types, $+$ and $\times$ are sum and product, $K \to \tau$ is exponentiation by a
  constant type $K$ (one with no free variables), and $\mu X.\,\tau$ forms the least
  fixed point, that is, the inductive type whose constructors are described by $\tau$
  with $X$ standing for recursive occurrences.  For example,
  $\N = \mu X.\, \mathbf{1} + X$, $\List\,A = \mu X.\, \mathbf{1} + A \times X$ and
  $\fn{Btree}\,A = \mu X.\, A + X \times X$.  See \cite{AbbottAltenkirchGhani05} for the
  construction of the container corresponding to each clause.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Container morphisms}\label{sec:morphisms}

A polymorphic function between container types, say $\rev : \List\,X \to \List\,X$, is
determined by what it does to shapes and positions: $\rev$ keeps the length, and the datum
at output position $i$ is the datum at input position $n - i - 1$.  Note the direction of
the second map: it goes from \emph{output} positions to \emph{input} positions.  This is
deliberate.  A polymorphic function cannot invent data, so every output position must be
filled from some input position --- hence a function from output positions to input
positions.  The opposite direction would not work in general: $\fn{tail}$ discards input
position $0$, which has nowhere to go (Figure~\ref{fig:tail}), and a function that
duplicates its input sends one input position to two output positions, which is not a
function at all.

\begin{definition}[Container morphism]\label{def:cmor}
  A \emph{container morphism} $(u, f) : \cont{S}{P} \lens \cont{S'}{P'}$ consists of
  \[
    u : S \to S' \qquad\text{and}\qquad f : \textstyle\prod_{s:S}\, P'\,(u\,s) \to P\,s .
  \]
  We call $u$ the \emph{shape map} and $f$ the \emph{position} or \emph{reindexing map}.
\end{definition}

The shape map is covariant (it follows the direction of the function) and the position map
is contravariant, for the reason just given.  Each container morphism determines a
polymorphic function, its extension:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mmap}
  \mmap\,(u,f) : \ext{\cont{S}{P}}\,X \to \ext{\cont{S'}{P'}}\,X, \qquad
  \mmap\,(u,f)\,(s, g) = (u\,s,\; g \circ f_s).
\end{equation}
In words: apply $u$ to the shape, and fill output position $p$ with the datum
$g\,(f_s\,p)$ found at the input position $f_s\,p$.

\begin{example}[Reverse]\label{ex:rev}
  $\fn{crev} = (\fn{id}, \fn{rv}) : (\N \triangleleft \Fin) \lens (\N \triangleleft \Fin)$,
  where $\fn{rv}_n\,i$ is the element of $\Fin\,n$ with value $n - i - 1$.  In the
  dependently typed style $\fn{rv}$ is defined by recursion on the index, using the
  functions $\fn{top}_n : \Fin(S\,n)$ (the largest element, of value $n$) and
  $\fn{emb}_n : \Fin\,n \to \Fin(S\,n)$ (the value-preserving embedding):
  \begin{align*}
    \fn{rv}_{S\,n}\,\fn{fz} &= \fn{top}_n &
    \fn{top}_0 &= \fn{fz} & \fn{emb}\,\fn{fz} &= \fn{fz}\\
    \fn{rv}_{S\,n}\,(\fn{fs}\,i) &= \fn{emb}\,(\fn{rv}_n\,i) &
    \fn{top}_{S\,n} &= \fn{fs}\,\fn{top}_n & \fn{emb}\,(\fn{fs}\,i) &= \fn{fs}\,(\fn{emb}\,i).
  \end{align*}
  Every element of $\Fin(S\,n)$ is either $\fn{top}_n$ or $\fn{emb}\,i$ for a unique $i$;
  this is a \emph{view} of $\Fin$ in the sense of \cite{McBride00,McBrideMcKinna04}, on
  which the dependently typed developments of \cite{PrinceGhaniMcBride08,Prince10} rely.
  The representation of \S\ref{sec:arith} does without it.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Tail]\label{ex:tail}
  $\fn{ctail} = (u, f)$ with $u\,0 = 0$, $u\,(S\,n) = n$, $f_0 = \;!$ (no output
  positions when the input is empty) and $f_{S\,n}\,i = \fn{fs}\,i$ (output position $i$
  is fetched from input position $i + 1$).
\end{example}

\begin{definition}[Cartesian morphism]\label{def:cartesian}
  A container morphism $(u,f)$ is \emph{cartesian} if every $f_s : P'(u\,s) \to P\,s$ is a
  bijection.
\end{definition}

A cartesian morphism rearranges data without loss or duplication; $\fn{crev}$ is
cartesian, $\fn{ctail}$ is not.  Note that cartesianness constrains the \emph{position}
maps only; the shape map $u$ may well fail to be injective
(Proposition~\ref{prop:flatten} gives an example).

\begin{definition}[Composition]\label{def:comp}
  Given $(u_1, f_1) : \cont{S}{P} \lens \cont{S'}{P'}$ and
  $(u_2, f_2) : \cont{S'}{P'} \lens \cont{S''}{P''}$, their composite
  $(u_2, f_2) \circ (u_1, f_1) : \cont{S}{P} \lens \cont{S''}{P''}$ is
  $(u_2 \circ u_1,\; \lambda s\,p.\; f_1\,s\,(f_2\,(u_1\,s)\,p))$.
\end{definition}

Shapes are mapped forwards through $u_1$ then $u_2$; a position of the final output is
routed backwards through $f_2$ then $f_1$.  Containers and container morphisms form a
category $\Cont$ \cite{AbbottAltenkirchGhani05}; $\mmap$ respects composition and
identities (\textsf{[Coq]} \coq{mmap\_comp}, \coq{mmap\_id}), and the composite of
cartesian morphisms is cartesian (\textsf{[Coq]} \coq{cartesian\_comp}).

\subsection{The representation theorem and equality}\label{sec:rep-eq}

\begin{theorem}[Representation \cite{AbbottAltenkirchGhani03,AbbottAltenkirchGhani05}]\label{thm:rep}
  The map $\mmap$ is a bijection between container morphisms
  $\cont{S}{P} \lens \cont{S'}{P'}$ and natural transformations
  $\ext{\cont{S}{P}} \to \ext{\cont{S'}{P'}}$.
\end{theorem}

Categorically the theorem is a consequence of the Yoneda lemma, and it says that
$T : \Cont \to [\Set, \Set]$ is a full and faithful embedding.  For us it says two things.
First, \emph{every} polymorphic function between lists is of the form
$\mmap\,(u, f)$, so nothing is lost by working with shape and position maps.  Second,
two polymorphic functions are equal exactly when their container morphisms are, so
proving equations between container morphisms is not only a sound way to prove equations
between polymorphic functions but a complete one.
(The core theory, including this theorem, was formalised in Coq in \cite[Ch.~4]{Prince10}.)

Stating the equality requires some care.  Two container morphisms
$(u, f), (u', f') : \cont{S}{P} \lens \cont{S'}{P'}$ have components of types
\[
  u, u' : S \to S', \qquad
  f : \textstyle\prod_s P'(u\,s) \to P\,s, \qquad
  f' : \textstyle\prod_s P'(u'\,s) \to P\,s .
\]
Two issues arise.  \emph{Heterogeneity}: $f$ and $f'$ have different types unless one
already knows $u = u'$, so the ordinary (homogeneous) equality of type theory cannot even
be used to state $f = f'$.  \emph{Extensionality}: we wish to regard two functions as
equal when they agree on all arguments, not only when they have the same definition.

The standard remedy for the first issue is McBride's heterogeneous equality
\cite{McBride99,McBride00}, written $a \jmeq b$ and also called ``John Major equality''.
Its rules are:
\begin{mathpar}
  \inferrule{a : A \\ b : B}{a \jmeq b : \fn{Prop}}
  \and
  \inferrule{a : A}{\fn{refl} : a \jmeq a}
  \and
  \inferrule{a : A \\ \Phi : \forall a' : A.\, a \jmeq a' \to \fn{Type} \\ \phi : \Phi\,a\,\fn{refl}}
            {\forall a' : A.\, \forall q : a \jmeq a'.\, \Phi\,a'\,q}
\end{mathpar}
These should be read as follows.  The formation rule says that $a \jmeq b$ is a proposition for
\emph{any} $a$ and $b$, even of different types --- so it can be \emph{stated}.  The
introduction rule says that the only way to \emph{prove} it is reflexivity, which requires
the same object on both sides, and hence the same type.  The elimination rule says that
a proof of $a \jmeq a'$ may be \emph{used} like a proof of ordinary equality, but only when
$a$ and $a'$ have the same type $A$.  Thus $\jmeq$ lets us write down equations between
objects of distinct types, while anything we can conclude from such an equation requires
the types to coincide.  The second issue is handled by comparing functions pointwise.

\begin{definition}[Equality of container morphisms]\label{def:eqmor}
  For $(u, f), (u', f') : \cont{S}{P} \lens \cont{S'}{P'}$ define
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:eqmor}
    \inferrule{\forall s : S.\; u\,s = u'\,s \\
               \forall s : S.\, \forall p : P'(u\,s).\, \forall p' : P'(u'\,s).\;
               p \jmeq p' \to f\,s\,p \jmeq f'\,s\,p'}
              {(u, f) \eqmor (u', f')}
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}

The first premise says that the shape maps agree.  The second says that the position maps
agree: for every shape $s$ and every pair of ``the same'' output position seen through
$u$ and through $u'$ (which is what $p \jmeq p'$ expresses, given the first premise), the
two input positions computed by $f$ and $f'$ coincide.  Since $f\,s\,p$ and $f'\,s\,p'$
both have type $P\,s$, the conclusion of the second premise is a homogeneous equality in
disguise.

\section{Containers as Agent Interfaces}\label{sec:agents}
% ===========================================================================

This section explains how the material of \S\ref{sec:containers} is read as a theory of
typed interfaces for AI agents.  Everything here is elementary; the point is to fix
definitions precisely enough that the verification results of \S\ref{sec:agents-verif}
can be stated.  The development owes much to Spivak and Niu's account of polynomial
functors as a ``mathematical theory of interaction'' \cite{Spivak20,NiuSpivak25}; polynomial
functors and containers are the same objects, and a container morphism is what
\cite{NiuSpivak25} calls a dependent lens.

\subsection{Interfaces}\label{sec:interfaces}

\begin{definition}[Interface]\label{def:interface}
  An \emph{interface} is a container $I = \cont{S}{P}$, read as follows: $S$ is the type
  of \emph{moves} (outputs) an agent with interface $I$ may emit, and for each move $s$,
  $P\,s$ is the type of \emph{responses} (inputs) the agent accepts after emitting $s$.
\end{definition}

In the notation of \cite{NiuSpivak25} the interface is the polynomial
$\sum_{s:S} y^{P\,s}$.  The definition takes the point of view of the party that moves
first.  The party on the other side of the interface, which we call the
\emph{environment}, receives a move and must answer with a response of the type that the
move dictates; that is the point of view of Ghani's observation in \S\ref{sec:intro}, in
which the moves are prompts.  A component of a larger system typically plays both parts,
answering on one interface and asking on others.  Some examples:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item An untyped language model is an environment for the interface
  $(\fn{String} \triangleleft \lambda\_.\,\fn{String})$: a string is sent, and any string
  may come back.
\item A tool-calling agent has interface $(\fn{Req} \triangleleft \fn{Res})$: it emits a
  request $r : \fn{Req}$ (a tool name with typed arguments) and accepts a result of the
  type $\fn{Res}\,r$ \emph{determined by that request}.  The dependency is the whole point:
  the response type depends on the move.  This is what structured-output and tool-use
  schemas \cite{OpenAIStructured24,MCP24} enforce at the boundary of an LLM.
\item The \emph{list interface} is the list container $(n : \N \triangleleft \Fin\,n)$:
  the agent emits a batch of $n$ items, and a response \emph{selects one of them} by its
  slot.  A ranked list of $n$ candidates from which the environment picks one is the
  typical case; \S\ref{sec:listshaped} adds data to the items and types to the responses.
\item A \emph{plan} is tree-shaped: a planner that decomposes a task into sub-tasks,
  recursively, emits a tree whose leaves are the atomic sub-tasks, and a response
  concerns one of the leaves (\S\ref{sec:trees}).
\end{itemize}

Interfaces can be combined.  If $I = \cont{S}{P}$ and $J = \cont{S'}{P'}$ are interfaces,
then so are
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item their sum $I + J$: emit a move of one or the other, and accept a response to it;
\item their product
  $I \times J = ((s,s') : S \times S' \triangleleft P\,s + P'\,s')$: emit a move of each,
  and accept a response to one of the two;
\item their parallel product
  $I \otimes J = ((s,s') : S \times S' \triangleleft P\,s \times P'\,s')$: emit a move of
  each, and accept a response to both; and
\item their composite
  $I \circ J = ((s,h) : \sum_{s:S} (P\,s \to S') \triangleleft \sum_{p : P\,s} P'\,(h\,p))$:
  emit an $I$-move whose positions carry $J$-moves; a response answers the $I$-move and
  then the $J$-move found at the position it chose.
\end{itemize}
Containers are closed under all of these
\cite{AbbottAltenkirchGhani05,NiuSpivak25}, with
$\ext{I \times J} \cong \ext{I} \times \ext{J}$ and
$\ext{I \circ J} \cong \ext{I} \circ \ext{J}$.  The product is the categorical one:
two morphisms $m_1 : I \lens J_1$ and $m_2 : I \lens J_2$ out of the same container have
a \emph{pairing} $\langle m_1, m_2 \rangle : I \lens J_1 \times J_2$, which is how a
function that uses its argument twice is expressed (\textsf{[Coq]} \coq{cProd},
\coq{cpair}).

The extension $\ext{I}\,X$ (Definition~\ref{def:extension}) has a natural reading as well:
an element $(s, g)$ of $\ext{I}\,X$ is a move $s$ together with a \emph{continuation}
$g : P\,s \to X$ that says what to do with each possible response.  For the list
interface, $(n, g)$ is a batch of $n$ slots each filled with an $X$ --- a list.  Both
readings are used below.

\subsection{Adapters}

\begin{definition}[Adapter]\label{def:adapter}
  An \emph{adapter} from interface $I = \cont{S}{P}$ to interface $J = \cont{S'}{P'}$ is a
  container morphism $(u, f) : I \lens J$.
\end{definition}

Unfolding Definition~\ref{def:cmor}: $u : S \to S'$ translates each move of $I$ into a
move of $J$, and $f_s : P'(u\,s) \to P\,s$ translates each response that $J$ may give
to the move $u\,s$ back into a response that the $I$-agent understands
(Figure~\ref{fig:lens}).  The contravariance of $f$, which was forced on us in
\S\ref{sec:morphisms} by the nature of polymorphic functions, is here forced by the
nature of protocols: an adapter that sits between an agent and its environment must
translate outgoing traffic forwards and incoming traffic backwards.  No ``lens laws'' are
imposed; the adapters of interest are rarely well-behaved in the sense of bidirectional
programming \cite{FosterEtAl07}, and none is needed.

\begin{example}\label{ex:adapters}
  (i)~\emph{Reversal of a ranking.}  A retriever exposes $k$ candidates best-first; a
  consumer expects worst-first.  The adapter is $\fn{crev}$ (Example~\ref{ex:rev}): the
  batch is re-indexed, and the consumer's choice of candidate $i$ is routed to candidate
  $k - i - 1$ in the retriever's numbering.
  (ii)~\emph{Context window.}  An agent exposes a history of length $n$ to a model that
  accepts at most $k$ turns.  The adapter $\lastk$ has shape map
  $u\,n = \min(n,k)$ and position map $f_n\,i = i + (n \monus k)$: window position $i$
  is history position $i + (n - k)$ when $n > k$, and $i$ otherwise.  A reference by the
  model to turn $i$ of what it was shown is thereby translated into a reference to the
  right turn of the history.
  (iii)~\emph{Fan-out and fan-in.}  An orchestrator splits a batch of $n$ requests at
  index $m$, dispatches the two parts, and reassembles the results.  Splitting is the pair
  of adapters $\fn{take}_m$, $\fn{drop}_m$; reassembly is $\app$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Agents, wrapping, and compositional verification}\label{sec:wrapping}

\begin{definition}[Agent]\label{def:agent}
  An \emph{agent} with interface $I$ and state type $X$ is a coalgebra of the extension
  functor: a function
  \[
    a : X \to \ext{I}\,X, \qquad a\,x = (\fn{readout}\,x,\; \fn{update}\,x),
  \]
  giving, for each state, the move the agent emits and, for each possible response, the
  next state.
\end{definition}

This is the familiar notion of a Moore machine with a dependently typed input alphabet,
and it is precisely a lens $X\,y^X \to I$ in the sense of \cite{NiuSpivak25}.  An
\emph{environment} for $I$ with state type $E$ is a function
\[
  e : E \to \textstyle\prod_{s:S}\, \mathbf{1} + (P\,s \times E)
\]
which, in each of its states, answers a move with a response and a next state, or
declines; it must decline a move that admits no response, such as the empty batch of the
list interface.  The state of the environment is whatever it remembers of the
interaction: a tool server may be stateless, a language model with a conversation is
not.  Running an agent from state $x_0$ against an environment in state $w_0$ produces
the sequence of moves $\fn{readout}\,x_0, \fn{readout}\,x_1, \dots$, where
$e\,w_j\,(\fn{readout}\,x_j) = (p_j, w_{j+1})$ and $x_{j+1} = \fn{update}\,x_j\,p_j$, and
it ends when the environment declines (\textsf{[Coq]} \coq{Env}, \coq{run}).  The run is
what the environment observes of the agent.  It records the moves only, not the states
and not the responses; this is deliberately the weakest reasonable notion of behaviour,
and it makes the completeness half of Theorem~\ref{thm:fullabs} the stronger.

\begin{definition}[Wrapping]\label{def:wrap}
  Given an adapter $m : I \lens J$ and an agent $a : X \to \ext{I}\,X$, the
  \emph{wrapped agent} $\fn{wrap}\,m\,a : X \to \ext{J}\,X$ is $\mmap\,m \circ a$.
\end{definition}

That is, the wrapped agent emits the move $u\,(\fn{readout}\,x)$ and, on response $p$,
moves to the state $\fn{update}\,x\,(f\,(\fn{readout}\,x)\,p)$.  The following properties
are immediate from the corresponding properties of $\mmap$.

\begin{proposition}[Compositionality of wrapping]\label{prop:wrap}
  \textsf{[Coq: \coq{wrap\_id}, \coq{wrap\_comp}, \coq{wrap\_ext\_eq}, \coq{run\_wrap\_pull}]}
  \leavevmode
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2em]
  \item $\fn{wrap}\,\fn{id}\,a = a$.
  \item $\fn{wrap}\,(m_2 \circ m_1)\,a = \fn{wrap}\,m_2\,(\fn{wrap}\,m_1\,a)$.
  \item If $\mmap\,m$ and $\mmap\,m'$ agree on the one-step behaviour of $a$, then
    $\fn{wrap}\,m\,a$ and $\fn{wrap}\,m'\,a$ produce the same run against every
    environment, for every number of steps.
  \item An adapter $m = (u,f) : I \lens J$ also acts on environments, in the opposite
    direction: an environment $e$ for $J$ yields the environment $m^{*}e$ for $I$ that
    answers the move $s$ with $f_s\,p$, where $p$ is the answer of $e$ to $u\,s$.
    Running $\fn{wrap}\,m\,a$ against $e$ and running $a$ against $m^{*}e$ are the same
    interaction: the first run is the image of the second under $u$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Item (ii) is the ``scalability'' dividend of types described in \cite{GhaniPodcast26}:
a pipeline of adapters is itself an adapter, and it is computed on the adapters alone.
Item (iii) is the ``trustworthiness'' dividend: to establish that two wirings of a system
have the same behaviour, it suffices to establish an equation between adapters, which by
Theorem~\ref{thm:rep} is an equation between shape and position maps.  Nothing need be
known about the agent itself --- in particular nothing about the language model that may
sit inside it, or inside the environment.  We are, with Ghani \cite{GhaniPodcast26},
verifying the plumbing, not the engine.  Item (iv) reconciles the two readings of an
interface: it makes no difference whether an adapter is regarded as part of the agent or
as part of the environment.

\subsection{List-shaped interfaces and lifting}\label{sec:listshaped}

The moves of the list interface are bare numbers: a batch of $n$ items of which nothing
more is said.  In practice the items carry data and expect typed answers, and the
interface is a composite.

\begin{definition}[List-shaped interface]\label{def:listshaped}
  Let $K = \cont{S_K}{P_K}$ be an interface, the \emph{item interface}.  The interface of
  \emph{batches of $K$-items} is the composite of the list container with $K$,
  \[
    \List \circ K \;=\; \big((n,k) : \textstyle\sum_{n:\N} (\Fin\,n \to S_K)
       \;\triangleleft\; \textstyle\sum_{i:\Fin\,n} P_K\,(k\,i)\big).
  \]
  A move is a list $k_0, \dots, k_{n-1}$ of $K$-moves; a response is a slot $i$ together
  with a $K$-response to the item $k_i$ in that slot.  An interface is
  \emph{list-shaped} if it is of this form.
\end{definition}

A batch of tool calls is $\List \circ (\fn{Req} \triangleleft \fn{Res})$: the agent emits
$n$ requests and the environment returns the result of one of them, tagged with its slot.
This is how results are returned in tool-use protocols, where each result carries the
identifier of the call it answers \cite{OpenAIStructured24,MCP24}; an environment that
answers all $n$ calls does so in $n$ rounds.  A conversation history offered to a model
is $\List \circ (\fn{Turn} \triangleleft \mathbf{1})$: a list of turns, in which a
response refers to one turn by its position (to cite it, or to regenerate from it).  A
list of ranked candidates is $\List \circ (\fn{Doc} \triangleleft \mathbf{1})$.  The list
interface itself is $\List \circ (\mathbf{1} \triangleleft \mathbf{1})$.  The list
morphisms of \S\ref{sec:containers} act on all of these at once.

\begin{proposition}[Lifting]\label{prop:lifting}
  \textsf{[Coq: \coq{cComp}, \coq{whisker}]}
  Let $m = (u, f) : (\N \triangleleft \Fin) \lens (\N \triangleleft \Fin)$ be a container
  morphism and $K$ an interface.  The \emph{lifting}
  $m \cdot K : \List \circ K \lens \List \circ K$ of $m$ has
  \[
    \text{shape map}\quad (n, k) \longmapsto (u\,n,\; k \circ f_n)
    \qquad\text{and position map}\quad (j, r) \longmapsto (f_n\,j,\; r) .
  \]
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2em]
  \item Lifting preserves identities and composition:
    $(m_2 \circ m_1) \cdot K = (m_2 \cdot K) \circ (m_1 \cdot K)$
    (\coq{whisker\_comp}).
  \item Under the isomorphism $\ext{\List \circ K}\,X \cong \List\,(\ext{K}\,X)$, the
    extension of $m \cdot K$ is the polymorphic function $\mmap\,m$ at the type
    $\ext{K}\,X$ (\coq{whisker\_assoc}).
  \item Consequently, if $m$ and $m'$ are equal in the sense of
    Definition~\ref{def:eqmor}, then $m \cdot K$ and $m' \cdot K$ send every batch to the
    same list of items, each with the same continuation (\coq{cmorEq\_whisker}).
  \end{enumerate}
  Moreover $m \cdot K$ is cartesian whenever $m$ is.  The same construction lifts a
  morphism $C \lens C'$ between arbitrary containers to $C \circ K \lens C' \circ K$.
\end{proposition}

Lifting is the operation called \emph{application} of a container morphism to a container
in \cite[Def.~3.8]{Prince10}; in $\Poly$ it is the action of the composition product on
morphisms \cite{NiuSpivak25}.  The shape map of the lifting is the extension of $m$
applied to the list of items: it is here that the polymorphic function denoted by $m$
acts on the \emph{contents} of a batch, while its position map routes responses.  Part (iii) is what allows the rest of the paper
to work with the bare list container: an equation proved there holds for batches of tool
calls, of turns, and of anything else.

\begin{remark}[Batches answered all at once]\label{rem:all}
  \textsf{[Coq: \coq{lift\_all}, \coq{lossless\_all}, \coq{flatten\_all}]}
  An agent that waits for the results of all $n$ items before it continues has an
  interface with the moves of $\List \circ K$ but with positions
  $\prod_{i : \Fin\,n} P_K\,(k\,i)$, one response for every item, in place of
  $\sum_{i:\Fin\,n} P_K\,(k\,i)$.  A list morphism $(u,f)$ does not lift to this interface
  in general: from results for the $u\,n$ items that were forwarded one cannot produce
  results for all $n$ items unless every item was forwarded.  What is needed is a section
  of the position maps, $g_n : \Fin\,n \to \Fin\,(u\,n)$ with $f_n \circ g_n = \fn{id}$;
  the result for item $i$ is then the one returned in slot $g_n\,i$.  A cartesian
  morphism has exactly one.  Losslessness is therefore more than an audit property: it
  is the condition under which an adapter can serve an agent that fans its results in
  together.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[A conversational agent and its window]\label{ex:conv-agent}
  Let the state of an agent be its history $h : \List\,\fn{Turn}$, and let its interface
  be $\List \circ K$ with $K = (\fn{Turn} \triangleleft \mathbf{1})$.  The agent $a$
  exposes its whole history, $\fn{readout}\,h = (|h|, \lambda i.\,h_i)$, and on receiving
  a response at position $i$ --- a request to regenerate from turn $i$, say --- discards
  the turns after $i$ and continues from there.  The model that serves the agent accepts
  at most $k$ turns.  Rather than change the agent, we wrap it:
  $\fn{wrap}\,(\lastk \cdot K)\,a$ exposes the $\min(|h|, k)$ most recent turns, with
  their contents, and routes a response at window position $i$ to history position
  $i + (|h| \monus k)$ --- the correct turn.  The identities of \S\ref{sec:worked} then
  say, for instance, that windowing an agent that is already windowed changes nothing,
  and that a window of a wider window is the narrower window, whatever the agent does
  with the responses it receives.
\end{example}

\subsection{Tree-shaped interfaces: plans and flattening}\label{sec:trees}

Not every interface is list-shaped, and a prominent example in agentic systems is the
\emph{plan}.  A planner decomposes a task into sub-tasks and those into sub-sub-tasks;
the result is a tree whose leaves are the atomic actions to be carried out, and the
planner expects a result for each leaf.  Trees with data at their leaves form the type
$\fn{Btree}\,X = \mu Y.\, X + Y \times Y$ of Remark~\ref{rem:shapes}, whose container is
\[
  \fn{Tree} \;=\; (s : \fn{TreeS} \triangleleft \fn{leaves}\,s),
  \qquad
  \fn{leaves}\,\fn{leaf} = \mathbf{1},\quad
  \fn{leaves}\,(\fn{node}\,l\,r) = \fn{leaves}\,l + \fn{leaves}\,r,
\]
where $\fn{TreeS} = \fn{Btree}\,\mathbf{1}$ is the type of tree skeletons and
$\fn{leaves}\,s$ is the type of leaf positions of $s$.  A value of
$\ext{\fn{Tree}}\,X$ is a skeleton together with an $X$ for each of its leaves, and the
leaves of a skeleton occur at any depth (Figure~\ref{fig:flatten}(a), after the picture
in \cite{AbbottAltenkirchGhaniMcBride04}).  The interface of a planner whose atomic tasks
have interface $K$ is the composite $\fn{Tree} \circ K$, formed exactly as for lists
(Definition~\ref{def:listshaped}).  Hierarchical delegation of this kind is studied in
depth by Libkind and Spivak \cite{LibkindSpivak24}, with polynomial functors as
task--solution interfaces and lenses as delegations; here we need only one adapter out of
it.

  \fn{flatten} = (\fn{Sum}, \fn{flat}) : \fn{Tree} \lens (\N \triangleleft \Fin),
  \qquad
  \fn{Sum}\,\fn{leaf} = 1,\quad \fn{Sum}\,(\fn{node}\,l\,r) = \fn{Sum}\,l + \fn{Sum}\,r,
\]
whose shape map counts the leaves and whose position map
$\fn{flat}_s : \Fin(\fn{Sum}\,s) \to \fn{leaves}\,s$ sends the $i$-th slot of the batch to the
$i$-th leaf in left-to-right order (Figure~\ref{fig:flatten}(b)): if $i < \fn{Sum}\,l$ the
slot belongs to the left subtree, and otherwise it is slot $i - \fn{Sum}\,l$ of the right
subtree.  This is the container morphism of the function that lists the leaves of a tree,
called \texttt{cflatten} in \cite[Listing~21]{Prince10}, and it has a property that
matters for agents.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:flatten}
  \textsf{[Coq: \coq{flatten}, \coq{flatten\_cartesian}, \coq{Sum\_not\_injective}]}
  $\fn{flatten}$ is cartesian: for every skeleton $s$, $\fn{flat}_s$ is a bijection, with
  inverse the left-to-right index of a leaf.  Its shape map $\fn{Sum}$ is not injective.
\end{proposition}

Read as an adapter, cartesianness says that wrapping a planner by $\fn{flatten}$ is
\emph{lossless}: the environment sees a flat batch of $\fn{Sum}\,s$ actions, every result it
returns is routed back to exactly one leaf, no leaf is left without a result and no result
is delivered twice.  Non-injectivity of the shape map says that the environment learns
nothing about the \emph{structure} of the plan --- two plans with the same number of leaves
present the same batch --- which is as it should be: the plan is the planner's business.
Cartesianness constrains positions, not shapes, and the distinction is exactly the one
between data (which an adapter may not lose) and structure (which it may hide).  By
Proposition~\ref{prop:lifting}, $\fn{flatten}$ lifts to
$\fn{flatten} \cdot K : \fn{Tree} \circ K \lens \List \circ K$ for every item interface
$K$, and since it has a section (the index of a leaf) it also serves planners that wait
for all of their results (Remark~\ref{rem:all}).

Flattening also connects tree-shaped interfaces to the decision procedure.  By
Proposition~\ref{prop:wrap}(ii), a list adapter $m$ applied after flattening is the single
adapter $m \circ \fn{flatten}$ out of the tree interface, so a planner can be windowed,
split across workers, or re-ordered using the adapters of \S\ref{sec:arith}; the
identities between those adapters, proved by the tactic of \S\ref{sec:coq}, hold of the
planner too, and a lossless list adapter after $\fn{flatten}$ is a lossless adapter out of
the tree interface (\S\ref{sec:worked}).  In the other direction, a tree adapter
$t : \fn{Tree} \lens \fn{Tree}$ (re-balancing a plan, say) can be compared with a list
adapter $m$ through the equation $\fn{flatten} \circ t = m \circ \fn{flatten}$, which asks
whether re-arranging the plan has the same effect on the batch as $m$.  Because the
position maps of $\fn{flatten}$ are bijections they can be cancelled, so the equation
determines $m$ from $t$ on every batch that arises from a plan.  Whether a decidable class
of tree adapters can be identified in this way is left open (\S\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\subsection{Borders and scope}\label{sec:borders}

An LLM produces text.  Two routes lead from text to a typed interface
\cite{GhaniPodcast26}: change the model so that it produces structured output natively,
or keep the model and check its output against a schema at the boundary --- ``passport
controls'', in Ghani's metaphor.  The second route is what structured-output APIs
\cite{OpenAIStructured24} and tool protocols \cite{MCP24} implement today: the schema is
a type, the parser is a checker, and a parse failure is a type error caught before it
propagates.  Our concern in this paper is not the parser but what happens after it, once
values of container types are flowing between components.  Nor do we claim anything about
the probabilistic internals of the model; the agent of Definition~\ref{def:agent} is an
arbitrary function of its state, and the environment an arbitrary function of its own.

Within that scope, the observation on which \S\ref{sec:agents-verif} rests is simple:
whenever the moves of an interface are lists --- of tool calls, of turns, of candidates
--- the interface is list-shaped, the adapters that rearrange such lists are liftings of
list morphisms, and list morphisms are the subject of \S\ref{sec:arith}.

\section{Arithmetic Verification}\label{sec:arith}
% ===========================================================================

We now turn to deciding equality between list morphisms.  We first show what a proof
looks like in the dependently typed setting (\S\ref{sec:dependent}) and why decision
procedures do not apply directly; then we derive a non-dependent representation
(\S\ref{sec:nondep}) and identify a decidable class (\S\ref{sec:presburger}).

\subsection{The dependently typed setting}\label{sec:dependent}

Consider proving that reversal is an involution.  In container terms the statement is
$(\fn{crev} \circ \fn{crev}) \eqmor \fn{id}$, and unpacking Definitions~\ref{def:comp}
and~\ref{def:eqmor} leaves two obligations:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item $\forall n : \N.\; n = n$, for the shapes, and
\item $\forall n : \N.\, \forall i : \Fin\,n.\; \fn{rv}_n\,(\fn{rv}_n\,i) = i$, for the
  positions.
\end{enumerate}
The first is trivial and the second is a lemma about $\Fin$ proved by induction on $i$.
Appending is similar but needs more machinery.  Its container morphism is
\[
  \fn{cappn} : ((n,m) : \N \times \N \triangleleft \Fin\,n + \Fin\,m) \lens (\N \triangleleft \Fin),
  \qquad u\,(n,m) = n + m,
\]
with the reindexing map $f_{(n,m)} : \Fin(n+m) \to \Fin\,n + \Fin\,m$ reflecting the
sum structure: output positions below $n$ come from the first list and the rest from the
second.  To define $f$ one needs the injections
$\fn{finl}_{n,m} : \Fin\,n \to \Fin(n+m)$ and $\fn{finr}_{n,m} : \Fin\,m \to \Fin(n+m)$
and a non-standard eliminator for $\Fin(n + m)$ that splits an element into its $\fn{finl}$
or $\fn{finr}$ component \cite{PrinceGhaniMcBride08}.  Proofs about $\app$ then proceed
by that eliminator.

This approach captures the arithmetic of list functions elegantly, but the definitions and
eliminators are non-trivial \cite{PrinceGhaniMcBride08}, each new function may need new
ones, and --- the decisive point --- the obligations are statements about the families
$\Fin\,n$, to which arithmetic decision procedures do not apply.  If instead we could
capture the \emph{behaviour} of the dependently typed reindexing maps by functions on
$\N$, decidable arithmetic reasoning would become possible.

\subsection{Towards non-dependent representations}\label{sec:nondep}

The type $\Fin\,n$ represents the set $\{ m : \N \mid m < n \}$, and it can be translated
to and from that set:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item $\fn{fnat} : \forall n.\, \Fin\,n \to \N$ computes the value of an element:
  $\fn{fnat}\,\fn{fz} = 0$ and $\fn{fnat}\,(\fn{fs}\,i) = S\,(\fn{fnat}\,i)$;
\item $\fn{nfin} : \forall n\,m.\, m < n \to \Fin\,n$ builds the element of value $m$
  (the case $n = 0$ is absurd, $m = 0$ gives $\fn{fz}$, and $S\,m < S\,n$ gives
  $\fn{fs}\,(\fn{nfin}\,n\,m\,\_)$ after peeling one successor off the proof);
\item $\fn{finnat} : \forall n.\, \forall i : \Fin\,n.\; \fn{fnat}\,i < n$ proves that
  values are in range.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{observation}\label{obs:one}
  Writing $\Fin\,n$ as $\{i \mid i < n\}$, a container morphism
  $(u, f) : (n : \N \triangleleft \{i \mid i < n\}) \lens (m : \N \triangleleft \{j \mid j < m\})$
  is a pair
  \[
    u : \N \to \N, \qquad f : \textstyle\prod_n\, \{i \mid i < u\,n\} \to \{j \mid j < n\},
  \]
  and an element of $\{i \mid i < n\}$ is a pair $(i, P)$ of a number $i$ and a
  \emph{proof} $P$ that $i < n$.  Equality \eqref{eq:eqmor} of two such morphisms
  $(u,f)$ and $(v,g)$ becomes
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:eqpairs}
    \inferrule{\forall n.\; u\,n = v\,n \\
               \forall n\,i\,j\,P\,Q.\; (i, P) \jmeq (j, Q) \to f\,n\,(i,P) = g\,n\,(j,Q)}
              {(u,f) = (v,g)}
  \end{equation}
  where $P : i < u\,n$ and $Q : j < v\,n$.  Note that the hypothesis
  $(i, P) \jmeq (j, Q)$ entails $i = j$; the proofs $P$ and $Q$ differ in type (one
  mentions $u$, the other $v$), which is why heterogeneous equality is still needed at
  this point.
\end{observation}

\begin{observation}\label{obs:two}
  Every reindexing map $f : \prod_n \Fin(u\,n) \to \Fin\,n$ has an \emph{arithmetic
  shadow}: a partial function on numbers, defined on $i < u\,n$, that returns the value
  of $f_n$ at the element of value $i$.  We call it $\reduce\,f$:
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:reduce}
    \reduce\,f\,n\,i\,h \;=\; \fn{fnat}\,(f_n\,(\fn{nfin}\,(u\,n)\,i\,h))
    \qquad (h : i < u\,n).
  \end{equation}
  Given the proof $h$, $\reduce\,f\,n\,i\,h$ is just a natural number, and
  $\reduce\,f$ corresponds exactly to $f$ while being stated without finite types.
  Consequently, for every $(u, f)$ there exists a total function $g : \N \times \N \to \N$
  that agrees with the shadow of $f$ wherever the latter is defined:
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:shadow}
    \forall n\,i\,(h : i < u\,n).\; g\,n\,i = \reduce\,f\,n\,i\,h .
  \end{equation}
  For reversal, $g\,n\,i = n - i - 1$, and one proves once and for all that
  $\forall n\,i\,h.\; \reduce\,\fn{rv}\,n\,i\,h = n - i - 1$.
\end{observation}

\paragraph{How the goal evolves.}  Observation~\ref{obs:two} lets us replace the second
premise of \eqref{eq:eqpairs} by arithmetic.  Suppose we have already proved $u = v$ and
are left with the position obligation.  Table~\ref{tab:goal} traces the transformation of
that obligation; in it, $g_1$ and $g_2$ are total functions satisfying \eqref{eq:shadow}
for $f$ and $g$ respectively.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \footnotesize
  \caption{Evolution of the position obligation.  Goal (e) mentions no finite types and
    no proofs.}\label{tab:goal}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llp{5.6cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    & Goal & Step \\
    \midrule
    (a) & $\forall n\,i\,j\,P\,Q.\; (i,P) \jmeq (j,Q) \to f\,n\,(i,P) = g\,n\,(j,Q)$
        & second premise of \eqref{eq:eqpairs}, with $P : i < u\,n$, $Q : j < v\,n$ \\
    (b) & $\forall n\,i\,j\,P\,Q.\; (i,P) \jmeq (j,Q) \to \reduce\,f\,n\,i\,P = \reduce\,g\,n\,j\,Q$
        & apply $\fn{fnat}$ to both sides (it is injective) and fold
          \eqref{eq:reduce}; the elements $(i,P)$ are now written $\fn{nfin}\,(u\,n)\,i\,P$ \\
    (c) & $\forall n\,i\,j\,P\,Q.\; (i,P) \jmeq (j,Q) \to g_1\,n\,i = g_2\,n\,j$
        & rewrite with the shadow equations \eqref{eq:shadow} for $f$ and $g$ \\
    (d) & $\forall n\,i\,j\,P\,Q.\; i = j \to P \jmeq Q \to g_1\,n\,i = g_2\,n\,j$
        & decompose the pair equation, using $u\,n = v\,n$:
          $(i,P) \jmeq (j,Q) \leftrightarrow i = j \wedge P \jmeq Q$ \\
    (e) & $\forall n\,i.\; i < u\,n \to i < v\,n \to g_1\,n\,i = g_2\,n\,i$
        & substitute $j := i$; the hypothesis $P \jmeq Q$ is now vacuous (both are proofs
          of $i < u\,n$) and is discarded, leaving only the range conditions \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The upshot is that, provided reindexing maps are given by their shadows, equality of
container morphisms between lists can be stated as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eq16}
  \inferrule{\forall n.\; u\,n = v\,n \\
             \forall n\,i.\; i < u\,n \to i < v\,n \to g_1\,n\,i = g_2\,n\,i}
            {(u, g_1) = (v, g_2)}
\end{equation}
in place of \eqref{eq:eqmor}.  We call \eqref{eq:eq16} \emph{quasi-container} equality.
It is stated purely in arithmetic, and it is sound: it implies that the two morphisms
denote the same function on lists (Theorem~\ref{thm:sound}) and, for morphisms whose
positions are in range, that they are equal as container morphisms in the sense of
Definition~\ref{def:eqmor} (Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge}).  If $u$, $v$, $g_1$ and $g_2$
lie in a decidable fragment of arithmetic, \eqref{eq:eq16} can be decided.  Which
fragment?  The shape maps of $\fn{crev}$ (identity) and $\fn{cappn}$ (addition) suggest
linear arithmetic, and we now show that this suggestion is right.

\subsection{Presburger-definable morphisms}\label{sec:presburger}

\emph{Presburger integer arithmetic} (PIA) is the first-order theory of the structure
$\langle \Z, 0, 1, +, \le \rangle$; its restriction to the natural numbers is
\emph{Presburger natural arithmetic} (PNA).  Presburger showed that PIA is decidable
\cite{Presburger29}, and the decidability of PNA follows (interpret $\N$ as the
non-negative integers).  Cut-off subtraction and conditionals on linear inequalities are
definable in PNA without quantifiers: the graph of $r = a \monus b$ is
$(b \le a \wedge r + b = a) \vee (a < b \wedge r = 0)$, and the graph of
$r = (\text{if } 0 < c \text{ then } x \text{ else } y)$ is
$(0 < c \wedge r = x) \vee (c = 0 \wedge r = y)$
(\textsf{[Coq]} \coq{suB\_graph}, \coq{pf\_graph}).

\begin{definition}[Linear and piecewise-linear functions]\label{def:lfun}
  A function $F : \N^k \to \N$ is \emph{linear definable} if it is denoted by a term of
  the grammar
  \[
    \fn{lin} \;::=\; \fn{const}\,c \;\mid\; \fn{var}\,j \;\mid\; \fn{add}\,\fn{lin}\,\fn{lin}
    \qquad (c \in \N,\; j < k)
  \]
  interpreted in the evident way: $\fn{const}\,c$ is the constant function,
  $\fn{var}\,j$ is the $j$-th projection and $\fn{add}$ is pointwise addition.  It is
  \emph{piecewise-linear definable} if it is denoted by a term of the grammar
  \begin{align*}
    \fn{lfun} \;&::=\; \fn{const}\,c \;\mid\; \fn{var}\,j \;\mid\;
       \fn{add}\,\fn{lfun}\,\fn{lfun} \;\mid\; \fn{sub}\,\fn{lfun}\,\fn{lfun} \\
    \fn{pl} \;&::=\; \fn{lp}\,\fn{lfun} \;\mid\; \fn{pf}\,\fn{pl}\,\fn{pl}\,\fn{pl}
  \end{align*}
  where $\fn{sub}$ is cut-off subtraction, $\fn{lp}$ is the inclusion and
  $\fn{pf}\,c\,l\,r$ denotes ``if $0 < c$ then $l$ else $r$''.
\end{definition}

Since a condition $a < b$ is the same as $0 < b \monus a$, nested $\fn{pf}$ expresses any
finite case analysis on linear inequalities, and $\min$ and $\max$ are definable.  The
second grammar is that of the Coq types \coq{lfun} and \coq{Plfun} of \S\ref{sec:coq}.

\begin{lemma}[Definability]\label{lem:qf}
  Let $t$ be a piecewise-linear term over the variables $\mathbf{x}$.  There is a
  quantifier-free formula $\delta_t(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}, r)$ of PNA, of size linear in
  that of $t$, with one auxiliary variable $z$ for each proper subterm of $t$, such that
  for all values of $\mathbf{x}$ there is exactly one choice of $\mathbf{z}$ and $r$ that
  satisfies $\delta_t$, and in it $r$ is the value of $t$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  By induction on $t$.  For $\fn{const}\,c$ and $\fn{var}\,j$ take $r = c$ and $r = x_j$;
  for $\fn{lp}\,e$ take the formula of $e$.
  For a compound term with immediate subterms $t_1, t_2$ (and $t_3$), take the conjunction
  of the formulae $\delta_{t_i}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}_i, z_i)$ of the subterms, with
  fresh result variables $z_i$, and one further conjunct: $r = z_1 + z_2$ for
  $\fn{add}$; the graph of cut-off subtraction displayed above, with $a := z_1$ and
  $b := z_2$, for $\fn{sub}$; and the graph of the conditional, with $c := z_1$,
  $x := z_2$ and $y := z_3$, for $\fn{pf}$.  Each of these conjuncts determines $r$
  uniquely from the $z_i$, which are determined uniquely by the induction hypothesis.
\end{proof}

Because the auxiliary variables are functionally determined, a statement
$\forall \mathbf{x}.\, \phi(t(\mathbf{x}))$ about the value of a term is equivalent to
$\forall \mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{z}\,r.\; \delta_t(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z},r) \to \phi(r)$,
which is again universal.

\begin{definition}[Linear and Presburger morphisms]\label{def:pmor}
  Let $(u, f) : (\mathbf{n} : \N^k \triangleleft \Fin\,n_1 + \dots + \Fin\,n_k) \lens (\N \triangleleft \Fin)$
  be a container morphism from $k$ lists to a list.
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2em]
  \item $(u, f)$ is \emph{linear} if $u$ is linear definable.
  \item $(u, f)$ is \emph{Presburger} if $u$ and the shadow of $f$ are piecewise-linear
    definable, where positions of the $k$ inputs are numbered consecutively, so that the
    shadow is a function $\N^k \times \N \to \N$ (output position $i$ to a position in
    $\Fin(n_1 + \dots + n_k)$).
  \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The word ``linear'' is sometimes used of container morphisms in another sense; ours
refers to the shape map, and we reserve ``cartesian'' for the morphisms of
Definition~\ref{def:cartesian}.  $\fn{crev}$ and
$\fn{cappn}$ are linear, with shape maps $\fn{var}\,0$ and
$\fn{add}\,(\fn{var}\,0)\,(\fn{var}\,1)$ respectively.

A linear shape map does not by itself make a morphism tractable, because it leaves the
position maps unconstrained.  The morphism that reverses a list when its length is prime
and returns it unchanged otherwise has the identity as its shape map, but its shadow is
not definable in Presburger arithmetic: the sets of numbers definable there are the
ultimately periodic ones, and the primes are not among them.  This is why clause~(ii)
constrains the position map as well.  What a linear shape map does determine is a division
of the output into blocks, one for each occurrence of a variable in the term, and the
morphisms that respect this division in the simplest way are Presburger.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:linear-pl}
  Let $u$ be a linear term built from variables and addition.  The morphism with shape
  map $u$ that fills the output, block by block and in order, with the inputs named by
  the occurrences of variables in $u$ is Presburger: its shadow is piecewise-linear
  definable, with one case distinction for each addition in $u$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  By induction on $u$.  For $\fn{var}\,j$ the output is the $(j+1)$-st input, and in the
  flat numbering of positions the shadow is $i \mapsto n_1 + \dots + n_j + i$.  For
  $\fn{add}\,l\,r$, an output position $i < u\,\mathbf{n}$ satisfies either
  $i < l\,\mathbf{n}$ or $i - l\,\mathbf{n} < r\,\mathbf{n}$; in the first case it belongs
  to the block of $l$ and in the second to that of $r$, so the shadow is
  \[
    \text{if } i < l\,\mathbf{n} \text{ then } g_l\,\mathbf{n}\,i
    \text{ else } g_r\,\mathbf{n}\,(i \monus l\,\mathbf{n}),
  \]
  where $g_l$ and $g_r$ are the shadows given by the induction hypothesis.  This is
  piecewise linear, because piecewise-linear terms are closed under substitution
  (\textsf{[Coq]} \coq{psubst\_ok}).
\end{proof}

For $u = \fn{add}\,(\fn{var}\,0)\,(\fn{var}\,1)$ the morphism of the proposition is
$\fn{cappn}$, and the shadow computed in the proof simplifies to the identity; for
$\fn{add}\,(\fn{var}\,0)\,(\fn{var}\,0)$ it is the function that doubles a list.  The
construction is that of \cite[\S6.2.1]{Prince10}, where it is carried out for the
dependently typed position maps; the statement of Proposition~6.2 there should be read
with the restriction made here.  Morphisms of this kind do not include $\fn{tail}$,
$\fn{head}$, $\fn{last}$ or $\fn{but\_last}$, whose shape maps involve subtraction, nor
$\fn{take}$, whose shape map is a minimum, nor indeed $\fn{crev}$, which traverses its
block backwards.  All of these are Presburger morphisms, however, and the Presburger
morphisms are closed under the operation that matters.

\begin{proposition}[Closure under composition]\label{prop:closure}
  If $(u_1, g_1)$ and $(u_2, g_2)$ are Presburger morphisms, so is their composite.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  By Definition~\ref{def:comp} the composite has shape map $u_2 \circ u_1$ and shadow
  $\lambda \mathbf{n}\,i.\; g_1\,\mathbf{n}\,(g_2\,(u_1\,\mathbf{n})\,i)$; piecewise-linear
  terms are closed under substitution of terms for variables, conditionals being
  propagated outwards (\textsf{[Coq]} \coq{psubst}, \coq{psubst\_ok}).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Decidability]\label{thm:decidable}
  Let $(u, g_1)$ and $(v, g_2)$ be Presburger morphisms.  The equality \eqref{eq:eq16}
  between them is decidable.  So are the \emph{range condition}
  $\forall \mathbf{n}\,i.\; i < u\,\mathbf{n} \to g_1\,\mathbf{n}\,i < n_1 + \dots + n_k$,
  and, for $k = 1$, the \emph{losslessness condition}
  \[
    \forall n.\; u\,n = n \;\wedge\; \forall i\,j.\; i < n \to j < n \to g_1\,n\,i = g_1\,n\,j \to i = j .
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Let $\delta_u(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{z}, r)$, $\delta_v(\mathbf{n}, \mathbf{z}', r')$,
  $\delta_1(\mathbf{n}, i, \mathbf{y}, r_1)$ and $\delta_2(\mathbf{n}, i, \mathbf{y}', r_2)$
  be the quantifier-free formulae that Lemma~\ref{lem:qf} provides for the terms denoting
  $u$, $v$, $g_1$ and $g_2$.  Then \eqref{eq:eq16} is equivalent to the PNA sentence
  \begin{align*}
    &\forall \mathbf{n}\,\mathbf{z}\,\mathbf{z}'\,r\,r'.\;
       \delta_u \to \delta_v \to r = r' \\
    \wedge\;\;&\forall \mathbf{n}\,i\,\mathbf{z}\,\mathbf{y}\,\mathbf{y}'\,r\,r_1\,r_2.\;
       \delta_u \to i < r \to \delta_1 \to \delta_2 \to r_1 = r_2 ,
  \end{align*}
  which is decidable by Presburger's theorem.  The other two conditions are
  translated in the same way.
\end{proof}

The proof shows more than decidability.  All three sentences are \emph{universal}: a
block of $\forall$s in front of a quantifier-free matrix, of size linear in the terms.
Deciding such a sentence amounts to deciding the unsatisfiability of a quantifier-free
formula, and satisfiability of quantifier-free Presburger formulae is NP-complete
\cite{Papadimitriou81}, whereas the full theory requires at least doubly exponential
nondeterministic time \cite{FischerRabin74}.  The universal fragment is exactly what the
Omega test \cite{Pugh91}, Coq's \coq{lia} and SMT solvers are built for, and this matters
for the practicality discussed in \S\ref{sec:coq}.  By contrast the dual of losslessness,
surjectivity of the position maps ($\forall n\,j.\, j < n \to \exists i < u\,n.\, g_1\,n\,i = j$),
has an existential quantifier and lies outside the fragment.  Happily, for functions
between finite sets of the same size injectivity implies bijectivity, which is why the
losslessness condition is stated as it is: for a morphism satisfying the range condition
it implies that the corresponding container morphism is cartesian
(Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge}).

\subsection{Representing Presburger morphisms}\label{sec:ppcm}

We now fix the concrete representation used in the development.  A Presburger morphism
of \emph{arity} $k$ (from $k$ lists to a list) is a pair of piecewise-linear terms
\[
  \mathsf{PPCm} \;=\; \fn{pl} \times \fn{pl},
\]
the first denoting the shape map over the variables $0, \dots, k-1$ (the input lengths),
the second denoting the shadow over the variables $0, \dots, k$, where variable $k$ is the
output position $i$.  Positions of the $k$ inputs are numbered consecutively (``flat''
encoding), via the isomorphism $\Fin\,n_1 + \dots + \Fin\,n_k \cong \Fin(n_1 + \dots + n_k)$.
A representation whose terms mention no other variables is \emph{closed}; all the
representations in this paper are, and closedness is checked by computation
(\textsf{[Coq]} \coq{closed}).

\begin{example}\label{ex:ppcm}
  Writing $x_0, x_1, x_2$ for the variables,
  \begin{align*}
    \fn{Rev} &= (x_0,\; (x_0 \monus x_1) \monus 1) &
    \fn{Tail} &= (x_0 \monus 1,\; x_1 + 1) \\
    \fn{Take}_m &= (\text{if } x_0 < m \text{ then } x_0 \text{ else } m,\; x_1) &
    \fn{Drop}_m &= (x_0 \monus m,\; x_1 + m) \\
    \fn{App} &= (x_0 + x_1,\; x_2) &
    \lastk &= (\text{if } x_0 < k \text{ then } x_0 \text{ else } k,\; x_1 + (x_0 \monus k)).
  \end{align*}
  Observe that with the flat encoding the position map of $\fn{App}$ is the identity: output
  position $i$ is input position $i$ of the concatenation.  (A position map that subtracted
  the length of the first list in its second case, as one might first write, would count
  twice the offset that the interpretation below already applies.)
\end{example}

The \emph{interpretation} of $(u, g) : \mathsf{PPCm}$ of arity $k$ on inputs
$(n_1, h_1), \dots, (n_k, h_k)$, where $h_j : \N \to X$ is the lookup function of the $j$-th
list, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:interp}
  \llbracket (u,g) \rrbracket\, \big((n_1,h_1),\dots,(n_k,h_k)\big) \;=\;
  \big(u\,\mathbf{n},\; \lambda i.\; \fn{flat}\,(g\,\mathbf{n}\,i)\big),
\end{equation}
where $\fn{flat}\,p = h_1\,p$ if $p < n_1$, $\fn{flat}\,p = h_2\,(p - n_1)$ if
$n_1 \le p < n_1 + n_2$, and so on (\textsf{[Coq]} \coq{interp}, \coq{flat}).  This is
$\mmap$ of \eqref{eq:mmap} for the flat encoding; a default value is returned for an
out-of-range position, which a morphism satisfying the range condition of
Theorem~\ref{thm:decidable} never produces.

\begin{theorem}[Soundness of quasi-container equality]\label{thm:sound}
  \textsf{[Coq: \coq{pcmEQ\_ok}]}  If $(u, g_1)$ and $(v, g_2)$ satisfy \eqref{eq:eq16},
  then for all inputs their interpretations have the same length and the same element at
  every position.
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[Composition of representations]\label{def:ppcomp}
  Let $l = (u_l, g_l)$ have arity $1$ and $r = (u_r, g_r)$ have arity $k$.  Their
  composite $\fn{comp}_1\,l\,r$, of arity $k$, is
  \[
    \big(\; u_l[x_0 := u_r],\;\; g_r[x_k := g_l[x_0 := u_r,\, x_1 := x_k]] \;\big),
  \]
  where $t[x := s]$ is substitution of piecewise-linear terms.  Let $l$ have arity $2$ and
  $r_1 = (u_1, g_1)$, $r_2 = (u_2, g_2)$ have arity $k$ \emph{over the same $k$ inputs}.
  Their composite $\fn{comp}_2\,l\,r_1\,r_2$, of arity $k$, has shape map
  $u_l[x_0 := u_1, x_1 := u_2]$ and, with $q = g_l[x_0 := u_1, x_1 := u_2, x_2 := x_k]$
  the position in the intermediate concatenation, position map
  \[
    \text{if } q < u_1 \text{ then } g_1[x_k := q] \text{ else } g_2[x_k := q \monus u_1] .
  \]
\end{definition}

The first clause is Definition~\ref{def:comp} written out for terms.  The second is the
composition needed for a binary function applied to two functions of the same argument(s)
--- for instance $\fn{take}_m\,b \app \fn{drop}_m\,b$, or $\rev\,ys \app \rev\,xs$ as a
function of $(xs, ys)$ --- and it is where the flat encoding earns its keep: the position
map is a case split on which intermediate list the position falls in.  (In the
development the terms substituted for $u_r$, $u_1$, $u_2$ are first \emph{shielded}, that
is, their variables $\ge k$ are set to $0$, so that a term of arity $k$ cannot observe the
output-position variable of the composite; this makes the following theorem exact rather
than subject to a free-variable side condition.)

\begin{theorem}[Correctness of composition]\label{thm:comp}
  \textsf{[Coq: \coq{comp1\_ok}, \coq{comp2\_ok}]}
  If $l$ satisfies the range condition, then for all inputs $\mathbf{x}$,
  \begin{align*}
    \llbracket \fn{comp}_1\,l\,r \rrbracket\,\mathbf{x} &= \llbracket l \rrbracket\,(\llbracket r \rrbracket\,\mathbf{x}) \\
    \llbracket \fn{comp}_2\,l\,r_1\,r_2 \rrbracket\,\mathbf{x} &= \llbracket l \rrbracket\,(\llbracket r_1 \rrbracket\,\mathbf{x}, \llbracket r_2 \rrbracket\,\mathbf{x}),
  \end{align*}
  up to equality of lengths and of elements at every position.
\end{theorem}

Equality \eqref{eq:eq16} is implemented as a proposition \coq{pcmEQ}, and statements of
it are proved by the tactic \coq{containers} described in \S\ref{sec:coq}.

\subsection{A closer look at some functions}\label{sec:closer}

The shape map of a function in this setting is exactly the shape map of its container
morphism, while its position map is the shadow of the container morphism's reindexing
map.  To represent a function one therefore states what its container morphism does to
lengths and indices, and the representation can usually be read off a whiteboard.

\paragraph{\fn{take} and \fn{drop}.}  $\fn{take}_m$ retains the first $m$ elements: if
$m < n$ the output has length $m$, otherwise $n$, and output position $i$ is always input
position $i$.  $\fn{drop}_m$ removes the first $m$ elements: the output has length
$n \monus m$ and output position $i$ is input position $i + m$.  Hence the representations
in Example~\ref{ex:ppcm}.  Note that $\fn{take}_m$ is \emph{not} cartesian: when $m < n$
positions $m, \dots, n-1$ of the input are dropped.

\paragraph{\fn{head}, \fn{last}, \fn{but\_last} and \fn{tail}.}  As functions
$\List\,X \to \List\,X$ (returning a list of length at most one, to stay polymorphic),
$\fn{head}$ and $\fn{last}$ have shape map $n \monus (n \monus 1)$, which is $1$ for $n > 0$
and $0$ for $n = 0$; the single output position is input position $0$ for $\fn{head}$ and
$n - 1$ for $\fn{last}$.  $\fn{but\_last}$ has shape map $n \monus 1$ and identity position
map; $\fn{tail}$ has the same shape map and position map $i + 1$.

\paragraph{Rotation.}  $\fn{rot}$ moves the first element to the back and
$\fn{unrot}$ moves the last element to the front.  Both keep the length.  Output position
$i$ of $\fn{rot}$ is input position $i + 1$ when $i < n - 1$ and $0$ otherwise; output
position $i$ of $\fn{unrot}$ is input position $i - 1$ when $i > 0$ and $n - 1$ otherwise.
In the dependently typed representation these require the view of $\Fin(S\,n)$ as
$\fn{top}$-or-$\fn{emb}$ (Example~\ref{ex:rev}); here they are two conditionals.

\subsection{The Coq development}\label{sec:coq}

The development consists of five files of about 3,100 lines in all.  It compiles with
Coq~8.18 and with the Rocq Prover~9.3, uses only the standard library, and contains no
axioms or admitted lemmas.

\paragraph{\coq{Piecewise.v}.}  The second grammar of Definition~\ref{def:lfun} is the pair
of inductive types
\begin{lstlisting}
Inductive lfun : Set :=                  Inductive Plfun : Set :=
  | cnst : nat -> lfun                     | lp : lfun -> Plfun
  | vr   : nat -> lfun                     | pf : Plfun -> Plfun -> Plfun -> Plfun.
  | adD  : lfun -> lfun -> lfun
  | suB  : lfun -> lfun -> lfun.
\end{lstlisting}
with evaluation functions \coq{evl} and \coq{pevl} against an environment
$\rho : \N \to \N$ (variable $j$ reads $\rho\,j$).  Variables are plain numbers rather
than elements of \coq{Fin n}, which removes every dependent pattern match from the
arithmetic core.  Substitution \coq{psubst} is defined with conditionals propagated
outwards, and proved correct.

\paragraph{\coq{ListMorphisms.v}.}  A morphism is a pair \coq{PPCm := Plfun * Plfun};
its arity is supplied to the equality and the interpretation rather than carried in the
type.  The file defines the interpretation \eqref{eq:interp}, the equality
\begin{lstlisting}
Definition pcmEQ (k : nat) (l r : PPCm) : Prop :=
  forall rho, pevl (shape l) rho = pevl (shape r) rho /\
    forall i, i < pevl (shape l) rho ->
      pevl (pos l) (ext rho k i) = pevl (pos r) (ext rho k i).
\end{lstlisting}
(where \coq{ext rho k i} binds variable $k$ to $i$), its soundness
(Theorem~\ref{thm:sound}), the range and losslessness conditions, composition
(Definition~\ref{def:ppcomp}) with its correctness (Theorem~\ref{thm:comp}), and the tactic
\begin{lstlisting}
Ltac containers := unfold_morphism; arith.
\end{lstlisting}
Here \coq{unfold\_morphism} unfolds the definitions, splits the conjunction and evaluates
the terms symbolically (rewriting with the correctness lemmas for substitution), and
\coq{arith} eliminates every remaining conditional by case analysis and calls \coq{lia}.
The tactic applies unchanged to the range and losslessness conditions.  Its soundness is
guaranteed by the kernel; the case analysis is exhaustive, so its completeness on these
goals rests on that of \coq{lia} for linear integer arithmetic.

A tactic that proves valid statements does not produce counterexamples to invalid ones,
so the file also provides a \emph{refutation} procedure for arity-1 morphisms.  The
function \coq{find\_cex l r b} searches the inputs of length at most $b$ for a length at
which the shape maps of $l$ and $r$ differ, or a length and a position at which their
position maps differ.  The search is exact:
\begin{lstlisting}
Theorem find_cex_spec : forall l r b,
    find_cex l r b = None <-> forall n, n <= b -> agree_at l r n.
\end{lstlisting}
where \coq{agree\_at l r n} says that $l$ and $r$ have the same shape and the same
position map on inputs of length $n$.  A counterexample that is found is therefore a
proof that the law fails (\coq{find\_cex\_sound}, tactic \coq{refute}), and a
counterexample that exists is found as soon as the bound reaches it.  Finally the file
relates \coq{pcmEQ}, which quantifies over all environments, to its restriction to the
environments that arise from $k$ inputs: for closed terms the two coincide
(\coq{pcmEQ\_on\_pcmEQ}).

\paragraph{\coq{Examples.v}.}  The representations of Example~\ref{ex:ppcm}, the
properties of Figure~\ref{fig:properties} and the adapter laws of
\S\ref{sec:agents-verif}, each proved by \coq{containers}.  A
representation is only as good as its agreement with the function it is meant to
represent.  A \fn{take} whose conditional is swapped, so that its shape map is
$\max(n,m)$ rather than $\min(n,m)$, satisfies both properties of
Figure~\ref{fig:properties} in which \fn{take} occurs (\coq{swapped\_drop\_take},
\coq{swapped\_take\_drop}).  It is caught by the range condition
(\coq{swapped\_take\_ill\_typed}), and, more to the point, each basic morphism is
validated against the standard library by a \emph{transfer lemma}, e.g.
\begin{lstlisting}
Lemma Take_spec : forall m (l : list X),
    RepEq (interp (Take m) [of_list d l] d) (of_list d (firstn m l)).
\end{lstlisting}
where \coq{of\_list} turns a list into a length-and-lookup pair and \coq{RepEq} is equality
of length and of elements at every position.  How hard is it to relate the container-style
definitions to the usual recursive ones?  A general criterion (\coq{spec1}) reduces each
transfer lemma to a comparison of lengths and of the elements at each index, which takes
between two and eight lines using standard facts about \coq{nth}, and it is done once per
function, not once per property.  With the transfer lemmas in hand, theorems about
ordinary lists follow from the decision procedure \emph{with no induction at all}: the
file derives, among others,
\begin{lstlisting}
Theorem rev_involutive_via_containers : forall l : list X, rev (rev l) = l.
Theorem firstn_skipn_via_containers :
  forall m (l : list X), firstn m l ++ skipn m l = l.
\end{lstlisting}
by chaining the transfer lemmas, Theorem~\ref{thm:comp}, Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} and the
relevant \coq{pcmEQ}.  This is a ``two-tier'' methodology: one recursive definition per
function for programming, one container representation per function for proving,
connected once.

\paragraph{\coq{Containers.v}.}  Containers, extensions, morphisms, $\mmap$ with its
functoriality, cartesian morphisms, products and pairing, composition of containers and
lifting, agents, environments, wrapping and runs, as in
\S\ref{sec:containers}--\ref{sec:agents}; equality of morphisms into the list container
(Definition~\ref{def:eqmor}, which for such morphisms needs neither heterogeneous
equality nor axioms, because the positions of the list container are numbers below a
bound); the bridge between the arithmetic representation and container morphisms, in
both directions, and between container morphisms and runs, in both directions
(\S\ref{sec:agents-verif}); and the tree container with the proof that flattening is
cartesian (\S\ref{sec:trees}).

\paragraph{\coq{AgentExamples.v}.}  The adapters of \S\ref{sec:agents-verif} as container
morphisms, their laws as equations between container morphisms and as statements about
runs, inequalities obtained from counterexamples, and concrete agents and environments
whose runs are computed by the kernel.

\paragraph{Performance.}  Table~\ref{tab:perf} gives proof times.  For comparison, the
\coq{omega}-based development of \cite{Prince10} took 6\,s for $\rev \circ \rev = \fn{id}$.
The present development proves every property of Figure~\ref{fig:properties} in at most
0.2\,s (all but two in under 50\,ms), and the most expensive statements it contains ---
equations between composites of several morphisms of arity~2, such as the
sliding-window invariants of \S\ref{sec:agents-verif} --- in about a second; all five
files compile in about ten seconds.  Three things account for the speed: \coq{lia}
(Micromega) replaces \coq{omega}, which was deprecated in Coq~8.12 and removed in 8.14;

universal form of the statements (Theorem~\ref{thm:decidable}) puts them in the
quantifier-free fragment for which these procedures are designed.  The cost grows with
the number of conditionals, since the tactic splits on each of them before calling
\coq{lia}; a term with $c$ conditionals may give rise to $2^c$ calls, which is the
price of proving the statement inside the proof assistant rather than trusting an
external solver.

\paragraph{Limitations.}  The procedure handles polymorphic functions
$\List(X)^k \to \List(X)$ whose container morphisms are Presburger; the bridge to
container morphisms and to behaviour (\S\ref{sec:agents-verif}) is mechanised for
$k \le 2$, which covers every example in the paper.  The term language has no division
by constants, so that functions such as taking every other element are not expressible,
although they remain within Presburger arithmetic.  Functions on nested lists, and
functions into other data types, are outside its scope; \S\ref{sec:conclusion} discusses
extensions.

\section{Verifying List-Shaped Agent Adapters}\label{sec:agents-verif}
% ===========================================================================

We now combine \S\ref{sec:agents} and \S\ref{sec:arith}.  Throughout, ``adapter'' means
a container morphism into the list interface given by a representation as in
\S\ref{sec:ppcm}; by Proposition~\ref{prop:lifting}, what is proved of such an adapter
holds of its lifting to batches of items of any interface.

\subsection{From arithmetic equality to agent behaviour}\label{sec:bridge}

The arithmetic representation lives on natural numbers; the agents of
Definition~\ref{def:agent} live on containers with dependently typed positions.  The
two are connected in two steps: from terms to container morphisms
(Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge}), and from container morphisms to behaviour
(Theorem~\ref{thm:fullabs}).

A morphism of arity $1$ has the list container as its source, and a morphism of arity $2$
the product $(\N \triangleleft \Fin) \times (\N \triangleleft \Fin)$, whose shapes are
pairs of lengths and whose positions are the positions of either list.  A representation
$t = (u, g)$ of arity $k$ \emph{represents} a container morphism $m$ from $k$ lists to a
list if $u$ computes the shape map of $m$ and $g$ the flat index of every value of its
position maps.

\begin{theorem}[From terms to container morphisms]\label{thm:bridge}
  \textsf{[Coq]}  Let $k \in \{1, 2\}$.
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2em]
  \item Every representation $t$ of arity $k$ that satisfies the range condition
    represents a container morphism $\widehat{t}$ from $k$ lists to a list; the range
    condition supplies the proofs that its positions exist (\coq{to\_cmor},
    \coq{to\_cmor2}).
  \item The extension of $\widehat{t}$ agrees with the interpretation
    \eqref{eq:interp} of $t$: reading a list off $\mmap\,\widehat{t}\,e$ gives the same
    length and elements as applying $\llbracket t \rrbracket$ to the list, or the two
    lists, read off $e$ (\coq{to\_cmor\_interp}, \coq{to\_cmor2\_interp}).
  \item Composition and pairing are respected.  If $l$ represents $m$ and $r$ represents
    $m'$, then $\fn{comp}_1\,l\,r$ represents $m \circ m'$; and if $l$, of arity $2$,
    represents $m$ while $r_1$ and $r_2$ represent $m_1$ and $m_2$, then
    $\fn{comp}_2\,l\,r_1\,r_2$ represents $m \circ \langle m_1, m_2 \rangle$
    (\coq{comp1\_represents}, \coq{comp2\_represents}).
  \item \emph{Soundness.}  If $t$ and $t'$ represent $m$ and $m'$ and
    $\fn{pcmEQ}\,t\,t'$ holds, then $m$ and $m'$ are equal in the sense of
    Definition~\ref{def:eqmor} (\coq{represents\_cmorEq}).
  \item \emph{Exactness.}  If moreover $t$ and $t'$ are closed, the converse holds as
    well: $\fn{pcmEQ}\,t\,t'$ if and only if $m$ and $m'$ are equal
    (\coq{pcmEQ\_iff\_cmorEq}).
  \item If $t$, of arity $1$, satisfies the range condition and the losslessness condition
    of Theorem~\ref{thm:decidable}, then $\widehat{t}$ is cartesian
    (\coq{lossless\_cartesian}).
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Parts (iii)--(v) make the method compositional, and complete, at the level of container
morphisms: to compare two pipelines of adapters one composes their representations and
calls the decision procedure once, and the answer is the truth about the pipelines, not
an approximation of it.  In particular, a counterexample found by the search of
\S\ref{sec:coq} is an inequality of container morphisms (\textsf{[Coq]}
\coq{find\_cex\_cmor}).  The development packages the two sources, one list and two, in
a small structure (\coq{Src}), over which (iii)--(v) are proved once; a further arity
needs only a further instance.

\begin{theorem}[Observational equivalence]\label{thm:fullabs}
  \textsf{[Coq]}  Let $m$ and $m'$ be adapters from an interface $I$ to the list
  interface.
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2em]
  \item If $m$ and $m'$ are equal, then for every agent $a$ with interface $I$, every
    environment, all initial states and every number of rounds, $\fn{wrap}\,m\,a$ and
    $\fn{wrap}\,m'\,a$ produce the same run (\coq{cmorEq\_run}).
  \item Conversely, suppose that equality of the positions of $I$ is decidable and that
    $I$ has a move that admits no response.  If $\fn{wrap}\,m\,a$ and
    $\fn{wrap}\,m'\,a$ produce the same run for every agent $a$ and every
    \emph{stateless} environment, then $m$ and $m'$ are equal (\coq{runs\_cmorEq}).
  \item Consequently, for adapters from one list, or from two lists, to a list, equality
    is observational equivalence; and for closed representations $l$ and $r$ of arity $1$
    that satisfy the range condition, $\fn{pcmEQ}\,l\,r$ holds if and only if
    $\widehat{l}$ and $\widehat{r}$ give every agent the same runs against every
    environment (\coq{list\_adapters\_iff\_runs}, \coq{list2\_adapters\_iff\_runs},
    \coq{pcmEQ\_iff\_runs}).
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  (i)~By induction on the number of rounds: equal shape maps give equal moves, so the
  environment gives the same response and moves to the same state, and equal position
  maps route that response to the same response of the agent, hence to the same next
  state of the agent.

  (ii)~Write $m = (u, f)$ and $m' = (u', f')$.  For the shape maps, the agent that emits
  the move $s$ whatever happens shows, in one round, that $u\,s = u'\,s$.  For the
  position maps, suppose that $f_s\,p \neq f'_s\,p$ for some move $s$ and some
  $p < u\,s$.  Let $s_0$ be a move without responses.  Then $u\,s_0 = 0$, because
  $f_{s_0}$ maps $\Fin\,(u\,s_0)$ into an empty type, whereas $u\,s > 0$.  Consider the
  agent that first emits $s$ and then, having received the response $r$, emits $s_0$ if
  $r = f_s\,p$ and $s$ otherwise, together with the environment that always selects the
  slot $p$.  Wrapped by $m$, the agent receives $f_s\,p$ and its second move is seen as
  $u\,s_0 = 0$; wrapped by $m'$, it receives $f'_s\,p$ and its second move is seen as
  $u'\,s = u\,s > 0$.  The two runs differ, contrary to the hypothesis.

  (iii)~The list container and its product with itself satisfy the hypotheses of (ii),
  with the empty batch, respectively the pair of empty batches, for $s_0$.  The last
  statement combines this with Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge}(v).
\end{proof}

Part (i) is the statement that an equation proved by \coq{containers} is a theorem about
\emph{behaviour}: whatever the agent --- an LLM-backed planner, a retriever, a
hand-written state machine --- and whatever the environment, the two adapters cannot be
told apart.  It holds for an arbitrary source interface, so it applies equally to a
planner served through $\fn{flatten}$.  Part (ii) says that no coarser notion of
equality would do: adapters that differ are told apart by a very simple agent, even
though a run records nothing but the moves that cross the interface.  With exactness, it
follows that the tactic \coq{containers} decides the observational equivalence of list
adapters, and that a law refuted by the search is observably wrong.

The hypothesis on $I$ in (ii) cannot be dropped.  The two adapters from the tree
interface of \S\ref{sec:trees} that present a plan as a batch of a single item --- its
leftmost leaf, respectively its rightmost leaf --- are different, yet every run through
either of them is a sequence of ones (\textsf{[Coq]} \coq{pick\_same\_runs},
\coq{pick\_differ}): there is no plan without leaves, and so an agent has no move with
which to make the difference visible.  When the items carry data, as in the liftings of
Proposition~\ref{prop:lifting}, the moves themselves reveal much more.  For the liftings
$m \cdot K$ the one-step version of (i) is Proposition~\ref{prop:lifting}(iii); the
statement about runs holds for them as well, but the moves of $\List \circ K$ contain
functions, and identifying two of them requires function extensionality, so the
development proves it under that hypothesis (\coq{cmorEq\_run\_lifted}) rather than
assume an axiom.

\subsection{Losslessness}

An adapter that drops or duplicates responses is sometimes intended ($\lastk$ must drop
old turns) and sometimes a bug (a fan-in that loses a tool result).  Either way it is a
fact worth knowing, and for governance and auditability it is a fact worth
\emph{checking}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:lossless}
  \textsf{[Coq: \coq{lossless}, \coq{lossless\_cartesian}, and the lemmas
  \coq{*\_lossless} and \coq{*\_lossy}]}
  Losslessness of an adapter is decidable (Theorem~\ref{thm:decidable}) and implies that
  the adapter is cartesian (Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge}(vi)).  Reversal and the two rotations
  are lossless, by \coq{containers}; $\fn{tail}$, $\fn{take}_m$, $\fn{drop}_{m+1}$,
  $\lastk$ and $\pink$ are not, and each is refuted by an explicit witness (a history of
  length $k + 1$ for $\lastk$).  Lossless adapters compose with one another and with
  $\fn{flatten}$ (\coq{cartesian\_comp}, \coq{lossless\_after\_flatten}), and they lift to
  batches that are answered all at once (Remark~\ref{rem:all}).
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Worked examples}\label{sec:worked}

The statements in this section are from the files \coq{Examples.v} and
\coq{AgentExamples.v}.  Each law is proved by the tactic \coq{containers}, or refuted by
\coq{refute}, in about a second or less (Table~\ref{tab:perf}).

\paragraph{Context windows.}  Let $h$ be a history and $t$ the new turns to be appended.
A sliding-window implementation must decide whether to truncate before or after
appending.  The procedure proves that it does not matter:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:window}
  \lastk\,(h \app t) \;=\; \lastk\,(\lastk\,h \app t),
\end{equation}
so a system may keep only the last $k$ turns between calls without ever diverging from a
system that keeps everything and truncates on demand.  In the representation,
\eqref{eq:window} is the equality of arity-2 morphisms
\[
  \fn{comp}_1\,\lastk\,\fn{App} \;=\; \fn{comp}_1\,\lastk\,\big(\fn{comp}_2\,\fn{App}\,(\lastk \text{ on } x_0)\,(\fn{id} \text{ on } x_1)\big)
\]
(\textsf{[Coq]} \coq{lastk\_app}).  Two further invariants are proved in the same way:
truncation is idempotent, $\lastk\,(\lastk\,h) = \lastk\,h$, and nested windows collapse
to the tighter one, $\lastk\,(\fn{last}_{k+1}\,h) = \lastk\,h$.  By
Theorems~\ref{thm:bridge} and~\ref{thm:fullabs} all three are equations between container
morphisms and statements about runs.  The invariant \eqref{eq:window} is the equation
\[
  \lastk \circ \fn{app} \;=\;
  \lastk \circ \fn{app} \circ \langle\, \lastk \circ \pi_1,\; \pi_2 \,\rangle
\]
between morphisms from a pair of lists to a list (\coq{window\_append}), so an agent
whose interface is a pair of batches --- a history and the new turns --- behaves in the
same way whichever implementation it is wrapped by (\coq{window\_append\_run}); and an
agent wrapped twice by the window behaves exactly as the agent wrapped once
(\coq{window\_idem}, \coq{window\_idem\_run}).

\paragraph{Shape maps do not determine behaviour.}  The adapters $\lastk$ and
$\fn{take}_k$ have the same shape map, $\min(n,k)$: through either of them an
environment sees batches of the same sizes.  They differ in their position maps, and
the difference is observable.  Consider the agent that in state $x$ emits a batch of
$x$ items and, when item $i$ is selected, moves to state $i$, and the environment that
always selects the last item it is shown.  Started in state $5$, the agent alone
produces the run $5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0$; wrapped by $\fn{last}_3$ it produces
$3, 3, 3, 2, 1, 0$; wrapped by $\fn{take}_3$ it produces $3, 2, 1, 0$ (\textsf{[Coq]}
\coq{run\_plain}, \coq{run\_window}, \coq{run\_firstk}, computed by the kernel; the
inequality of the two adapters is \coq{window\_ne\_firstk}).  Equality of adapters must
therefore compare position maps, which is what Definition~\ref{def:eqmor} and its
arithmetic form \eqref{eq:eq16} do.

\paragraph{Pinning the first turn.}  Practical context windows keep the first turn of a
conversation, the system prompt, together with the most recent turns.  The plain window
does not: the law $\fn{head}\,(\lastk\,h) = \fn{head}\,h$ is false, and the search returns
the smallest counterexample, a history of length $3$ for $k = 2$
(\coq{head\_lastk\_cex}).  The adapter $\pink$ that keeps the first turn and the last $k$
has
\[
  u\,n = \min(n, k+1), \qquad
  f_n\,i = \text{if } i < 1 \text{ then } 0 \text{ else } i + (n \monus (k+1)) .
\]
For it the procedure proves that the first turn is never lost,
$\fn{head}\,(\pink\,h) = \fn{head}\,h$; that it is idempotent; that
$\pink\,(\fn{pin}_{k+1}\,h) = \pink\,h$; and the sliding invariant
$\pink\,(h \app t) = \pink\,(\pink\,h \app t)$ (\coq{head\_pin}, \coq{pin\_idem},
\coq{pin\_pin}, \coq{pin\_app}).  Its transfer lemma relates it to the evident function
on lists (\coq{Pin\_spec}).  It has the shape map of $\fn{last}_{k+1}$ and is a different
adapter (\coq{window\_ne\_pinned}): an environment that always answers the first item it
is shown has that answer routed to the first turn by the one and to the oldest visible
turn by the other, and the agent of the previous paragraph, started in state $5$, then
produces the run $3, 0$ through $\fn{pin}_2$ and the run $3, 2, 0$ through
$\fn{last}_3$ (\coq{run\_pinned\_first}, \coq{run\_window\_first}).

\paragraph{A refuted law.}  It is tempting to ``optimise'' the window by dropping the
oldest turn of a $(k+1)$-window, on the grounds that
$\fn{drop}_1\,(\fn{last}_{k+1}\,h) = \lastk\,h$.  This is false when the history is shorter
than the window: for a history of length $1$ and $k = 1$ the left side is empty and the
right side is not.  The search finds exactly that witness (\textsf{[Coq]}
\coq{drop\_lastk\_cex}, \coq{drop\_lastk\_refuted}).  Likewise an off-by-one position
map, $f_n\,i = i + (n \monus k) + 1$, is rejected for $k = 2$ with the witness $n = 1$,
$i = 0$ (\coq{badlastk\_cex}, \coq{badlastk\_detected}); this adapter is not even well
typed, for it fails the range condition (\coq{badlastk\_ill\_typed}).  A false adapter
law is a routing bug: the agent would be shown the wrong turn.  By
Theorem~\ref{thm:fullabs} it is also an observable one: it is not the case that every
agent behaves in the same way under $\fn{drop}_1 \circ \fn{last}_2$ and under
$\fn{last}_1$ (\coq{drop\_window\_observable}).  That true laws are proved and false ones
refuted with concrete counterexamples, in both cases without guidance, is for this
application the essential property.

\paragraph{Fan-out and fan-in.}  An orchestrator that splits a batch of $n$ requests at
index $m$ and reassembles the $n$ results must restore the original correspondence between
requests and results.  The procedure proves
$\fn{take}_m\,b \app \fn{drop}_m\,b = b$ as an equation between the arity-1 morphisms
$\fn{comp}_2\,\fn{App}\,\fn{Take}_m\,\fn{Drop}_m$ and $\fn{id}$
(\textsf{[Coq]} \coq{split\_merge}): result $i$ is routed back to request $i$.  As an
equation between container morphisms it reads
$\fn{app} \circ \langle \fn{take}_m, \fn{drop}_m \rangle = \fn{id}$
(\coq{split\_merge\_cmor}).  The method scales: a tree of splits and merges is a
composite of representations, and its equality to the identity is one \coq{pcmEQ}; the
three-way split
$\fn{take}_a\,b \app (\fn{take}_c\,(\fn{drop}_a\,b) \app \fn{drop}_{a+c}\,b) = b$ is
proved in the same way (\coq{split3\_merge}, \coq{split3\_merge\_cmor}).

\paragraph{Typed tool calls.}  To see a lifted adapter at work on an interface whose
responses are typed, let the item interface be
$\fn{Tool} = (\fn{req} \triangleleft \fn{res})$, with two requests, one asking for a
number and one for a truth value, and $\fn{res}$ the corresponding type of results.  An
agent holds a list of pending requests, emits them as a batch, and when the result of one
of them arrives --- a slot together with a result of the type that the request in that
slot demands --- removes that request from its list.  Its environment executes the first
call of whatever batch it is shown.  Started with the requests for a number, a truth
value and a number, the agent emits batches of three, two, one and no requests.  Behind
the lifted window $\fn{last}_2 \cdot \fn{Tool}$ the environment sees only the last two
requests and answers the first of them, the request for a truth value, with a truth
value; the adapter routes the answer to the second request of the agent's list, where
its type is the right one, and the batches seen by the environment are those of the
requests (truth value, number), (number, number), (number) and none (\textsf{[Coq]}
\coq{run\_caller}, \coq{run\_caller\_window}).  That the routed answer is well typed is
not checked at run time: it is the typing of the position map of the lifting.

\paragraph{Hierarchical planners.}  A planner on the tree interface of
\S\ref{sec:trees}, wrapped by $\fn{flatten}$, is an agent on the list interface, and the
results above apply to it unchanged: list adapters that are equal in the arithmetic
sense give the planner the same runs (\coq{pcmEQ\_run\_planner}), and in particular
windowing a flattened planner twice is the same as windowing it once
(\coq{planner\_window\_idem}).  A pipeline that flattens a plan, reverses the batch and
rotates it is cartesian, because each of its stages is (\coq{pipeline\_cartesian}):
every result is routed to exactly one leaf and every leaf receives one.  For the plan
of Figure~\ref{fig:flatten}, whose leaves lie at different depths, the kernel computes
that the three slots of the batch are routed to the leaves $x_1$, $x_2$ and $x_3$ in
that order (\coq{flatten\_figure}).

\paragraph{Reorderings.}  The identities of Figure~\ref{fig:properties} read, in the
agent setting, as facts about rankings and histories: ``the top candidate of the reversed
ranking is the bottom candidate of the original'' ($\fn{head} \circ \rev = \fn{last}$),
``reversing twice is the identity'', ``rotating a round-robin schedule forward and then
back changes nothing'' ($\fn{unrot} \circ \fn{rot} = \fn{id}$).

\subsection{Generate and check}\label{sec:generate-check}

Could the translation from a recursive definition to its container representation be
automated?  Two observations bear on the question.  First, the thesis
\cite[\S7.2.3]{Prince10} already gives a \emph{semantic} translation: for any polymorphic
$F : \forall X.\, \List\,X \to \List\,X$, Theorem~\ref{thm:rep} yields its container
morphism, and $\reduce$ \eqref{eq:reduce} yields the shadow; what is not automatic is
recognising the shadow as a piecewise-linear \emph{term}, which is a normalisation problem
rather than a proof problem.  Second, the division of labour between an untrusted
\emph{proposer} and a trusted \emph{checker} has become the standard architecture for
combining language models with proof assistants \cite{Harmonic25,AlphaProof25}, and it
fits here exactly: a model may propose the representation $(u, g)$ of a function together
with its transfer lemma, and the proof of the transfer lemma (a few lines) and all
subsequent properties (proved by \coq{containers}) are checked by Coq.  Ghani's remark
that a current language model resembles a precocious doctoral student, who works hard
and is quite often wrong \cite{GhaniPodcast26}, is precisely the reason the checker must
be a proof assistant and not another model.  A natural objection to the container
style --- that index arithmetic is an unnatural way to program lists, closer to arrays
in C than to lists in a functional language --- is in this domain inverted: index
arithmetic on histories and batches (\texttt{history[-k:]}, \texttt{results[i]}) is how
orchestration code is written today.  What is missing is the proof that the arithmetic
is right, which is what the procedure supplies.

\subsection{What this does and does not establish}

The results above are about the composition of components along typed, list-shaped
interfaces.  They say nothing about whether a language model will produce a parseable
output (that is the parser's job), nothing about the model's internals, and nothing about
interfaces whose moves are not lists, beyond what can be reached through a morphism such
as $\fn{flatten}$.  Within their scope they deliver, for the list fragment, three things
that Kodamai's ``math-first'' programme asks of enterprise agents
\cite{KodamaiEEJ26,KodamaiPR26}: adapters are verified rather than tested, so that an
error in the plumbing cannot propagate silently; interfaces are typed contracts specific
to the data flowing through them; and a component can be exchanged for another with the
same interface, with the equations between adapters guaranteeing that the exchange
preserves behaviour.  Extending the decidable fragment beyond lists --- to trees, to
interfaces indexed by several counts, to the mode-dependent interfaces of
\cite{Spivak26} --- is the natural next step, and \S\ref{sec:conclusion} outlines it.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
% ===========================================================================

\paragraph{Containers and polynomial functors.}  Containers were introduced by Abbott,
Altenkirch and Ghani \cite{Abbott03,AbbottAltenkirchGhani03,AbbottAltenkirchGhani05} as a
semantic account of strictly positive data types, and extended to indexed containers in
\cite{AltenkirchGhaniHancockMcBrideMorris15}.  Polynomial functors, the same objects in a
categorical dress, were studied by Gambino and Kock \cite{GambinoKock13} and taken up by
Spivak as a setting for ``mode-dependent dynamics'' \cite{Spivak20}; the monograph of Niu
and Spivak \cite{NiuSpivak25} develops the category $\Poly$ systematically, including the
reading of lenses as interfaces of interacting systems that \S\ref{sec:agents} adopts.
Libkind and Spivak \cite{LibkindSpivak24} use polynomial functors to model hierarchical
task delegation between agents, with morphisms as delegations; our adapters are the
special case of list-shaped interfaces, and our contribution is the decision procedure
for that case.  Spivak's recent work on interfaces that change during interaction
\cite{Spivak26} suggests where a richer decidable fragment might be sought.  Lenses as
bidirectional transformations go back to the view-update problem \cite{FosterEtAl07};
their use in compositional game theory \cite{GhaniHedgesWinschelZahn18} and categorical cybernetics
\cite{CapucciGavranovicHedgesRischel22} is the nearest precedent for ``adapters that route
information backwards''.

\paragraph{Containers in reasoning.}  Containers were used to construct polymorphic
programs with quotient types in \cite{AbbottAltenkirchGhaniMcBride04}, and as the
underpinning of the ellipsis technique in \cite{PrinceGhaniMcBride08}, of which the
present paper is a continuation.  Generic programming with containers and universes is
treated in \cite{AltenkirchMcBrideMorris07,Morris07}.

\paragraph{Typed agentic AI.}  The gap between untyped LLM output and typed components is
addressed in practice by structured-output APIs that constrain generation to a JSON schema
\cite{OpenAIStructured24} and by tool protocols such as the Model Context Protocol
\cite{MCP24}, which type tool invocations and results.  Kodamai's Calvin Grove platform
\cite{KodamaiEEJ26} composes agents as modules that communicate through typed
interfaces, which it calls contracts, with ``meta-agents'' above the individual agents;
Ghani describes its type system as built on containers, with agents as first-class
values that can be passed to and returned by other agents \cite{GhaniPodcast26}.  The present paper supplies a
decision procedure for one fragment of such a type system.  On the proof side, Harmonic's
Aristotle \cite{Harmonic25} and DeepMind's AlphaProof \cite{AlphaProof25} produce
Lean proofs checked by the kernel; \S\ref{sec:generate-check} places our procedure in the
same proposer/checker architecture.

\paragraph{Decision procedures for data structures.}  Suter, Dotta and Kuncak
\cite{SuterDottaKuncak10} extend quantifier-free constraints on algebraic data types with
catamorphisms, allowing reasoning about the relationship between a data structure and the
value of a recursive function applied to it.  Zhang, Sipma and Manna \cite{ZhangSipmaManna04}
combine recursive data structures with Presburger arithmetic through size functions.
Bouajjani et al.\ \cite{BouajjaniDrEnSi09} define a logic for composite data structures
with a decidable satisfiability problem.  Ghilardi et al.\ \cite{GhilardiNRZ07} extend the
theory of arrays with Presburger-definable indices; Bradley, Manna and Sipma
\cite{BradleyMannaSipma06} identify a decidable fragment of the theory of arrays,
extended to infinite arrays by Habermehl, Iosif and Vojnar \cite{HabermehlIosifVojnar08}
using automata \cite{Buchi60}.  Arons et al.\ \cite{AronsPRXZ01} verify parameterized systems
by automatically computed inductive assertions.  Our procedure differs from all of these in
its object: not the values stored in a structure but the \emph{polymorphic} functions
between structures, obtained through the representation theorem; the arithmetic is on
lengths and indices alone.

\paragraph{Presburger arithmetic in verification.}  Presburger's theorem \cite{Presburger29}
and its rational analogue \cite{KreiselKrivine67} underlie a large number of tools.  The
Omega test \cite{Pugh91} has been used for timing verification \cite{AmonBHL97} and
datapath verification \cite{BrinkmannDrechsler02,JohannsenDrechsler01}.  In proof
assistants, Coq's \coq{omega} has been superseded by the certificate-checking \coq{lia}
of the Micromega plugin, and Lean~4 acquired an \coq{omega} decision procedure in 2024
\cite{Lean470}; the procedure of this paper is independent of the choice and should port
to Lean directly.  The canonical form for piecewise-linear functions of Chua and Kang
\cite{ChuaKang77}, used in \cite[Ch.~5]{Prince10} to decide equality of piecewise-linear
functions over ordered fields, is not needed here because the statements of
Theorem~\ref{thm:decidable} are handled directly by the arithmetic procedure; Carette
\cite{Carette07} gives canonical forms for more general piecewise functions.

\section{Conclusion and Further Work}\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

We have shown how the container representation of polymorphic functions between lists can
be reduced to arithmetic on natural numbers, how equality of such functions becomes a
universal sentence of Presburger arithmetic when the shape and position maps are
piecewise linear, and how the resulting decision procedure proves the usual identities
between list functions without induction.  We have then read the same mathematics as a
theory of typed agent interfaces: container morphisms are adapters, wrapping is
functorial, and for list-shaped interfaces equality of adapters is decidable and
coincides with observational equivalence, losslessness is decidable, and both lift to
lists of typed items.  All of this is mechanised, without axioms.

Several directions remain.

\paragraph{Nested lists and other data types.}  The procedure handles
$\List(X)^k \to \List(X)$ only.  For nested lists the shape of the inner lists is a vector
of lengths; extending the term language so that $\fn{var}$ ranges over a vector of
vectors is straightforward syntactically, but the resulting position maps need
quantification over the inner index and may leave the universal fragment.  For other data
types a promising starting point is the data types with a \emph{cartesian} morphism into
lists, of which flattening (\S\ref{sec:trees}) is the paradigm.  Through such a morphism,
reindexing maps between trees may be representable as reindexing maps between lists, and
hence decided: an equation between tree adapters whose position maps are determined by
their flattenings would reduce to an equation between list adapters, and the question is
for which classes of tree adapters this reduction is complete.  On the list side, the
bridge of Theorem~\ref{thm:bridge} is mechanised for one and for two lists; a further
arity needs only a further instance of the structure over which it is proved.

\paragraph{Full Presburger arithmetic.}  Properties with existential content ---
surjectivity of a position map, or the existence of a split point with some property ---
leave the universal fragment.  They remain decidable, by Cooper's algorithm
\cite{Cooper72} or by automata-based procedures, at a higher cost.  An implementation along the lines of
\cite{HabermehlIosifVojnar08} would extend the range of adapter properties that can be
checked.

\paragraph{Higher-order adapters.}  $\Poly$ is closed for the parallel product
\cite{NiuSpivak25}: there is an interface $[I, J]$ whose moves are the adapters from $I$
to $J$, and ``meta-agents'' that take agents as inputs and return agents
\cite{KodamaiEEJ26,GhaniPodcast26} are naturally agents on such interfaces.  Our procedure
decides equality of the moves of $[I, J]$ in the list-shaped, Presburger fragment; a
natural question is whether a decidable fragment of the full internal hom can be
identified, which would make the typing of meta-agents checkable rather than merely
expressible.

\paragraph{Mode-dependent interfaces.}  Interfaces whose positions depend on the
history of the interaction \cite{Spivak26} are the general case of a context window: the
window's shape at step $t$ depends on everything emitted so far.  The window invariants of
\S\ref{sec:worked} are simple instances of equations about such history-dependent
presentations that can be decided; characterising a decidable fragment is open.

\paragraph{Richer observations.}  Theorem~\ref{thm:fullabs} observes only the moves that
cross an interface, and its completeness half relies on the empty batch.  For interfaces
without an empty move, such as that of plans, and for the list-shaped interfaces
$\List \circ K$, whose moves carry the data of the items, the right notion of
observation, and the corresponding completeness theorem, remain to be worked out.

\paragraph{Portability.}  Nothing in the development depends on Coq specifically.  A Lean~4
port, using its \coq{omega} tactic, would bring the procedure to the proof assistant in
which much machine-generated mathematics is currently checked
\cite{Harmonic25,AlphaProof25}.
